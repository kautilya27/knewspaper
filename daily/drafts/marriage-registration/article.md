**Quick answer:** If you and your spouse are both Hindu, Buddhist, Jain or Sikh and have already had a religious wedding, you register it under the **Hindu Marriage Act, 1955** at your district's marriage registrar or sub-registrar, usually with two witnesses and no waiting period. If you belong to different religions, don't want a religious ceremony, or simply prefer a civil marriage, you use the **Special Marriage Act, 1954**, which needs a written notice, a **30-day** period for objections and **three witnesses**.

In most states you start online on the state portal (Delhi's e-District, UP's IGRSUP, Tamil Nadu's TNREGINET and so on) and then visit the office once, together, with your witnesses. The certificate you get is the document that proves your marriage to passport offices, foreign embassies, banks and insurers.

## Is marriage registration compulsory in India?

In practice, yes. In *Seema v. Ashwani Kumar* (14 February 2006), the Supreme Court held that marriages of all Indian citizens, whatever their religion, should be compulsorily registrable in the state where they take place, and directed states to notify registration rules. Tamil Nadu, for example, made registration compulsory under the Tamil Nadu Registration of Marriages Act, 2009, with couples expected to apply within 90 days of the wedding, according to *Deccan Herald*'s report on the rules. Delhi's Compulsory Registration of Marriage Order, 2014 asks for registration within 60 days, according to the New Delhi District Magistrate's office.

Note that under Section 8(5) of the Hindu Marriage Act, a Hindu marriage is not invalid just because it wasn't registered. Registration is proof, not the marriage itself, but it is the proof everyone will ask for.

## Hindu Marriage Act vs Special Marriage Act: the real differences

| | Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 | Special Marriage Act, 1954 |
|---|---|---|
| Who can use it | Both partners Hindu, Buddhist, Jain or Sikh | Any two Indian citizens, whatever their religion (also used by same-religion couples who want a civil marriage) |
| When you apply | After the religious wedding | Before the marriage (Section 5), or after a wedding in another form (Sections 15 and 16) |
| Minimum age | Groom 21, bride 18 | Groom 21, bride 18 (Section 4); for registering an earlier wedding under Section 15, both must be 21 |
| Residence rule | Depends on state rules; usually one partner must live in the registrar's district | At least one partner must have lived in the Marriage Officer's district for 30 days before the notice |
| Notice and waiting period | None in the Act | Notice put up in the Marriage Officer's office; anyone can object within 30 days |
| Witnesses | Two in most states (Delhi lists two) | Three, who sign the declaration with you |
| Wedding ceremony | The religious ceremony comes first | The marriage is solemnised before the Marriage Officer in any form, using words set by the Act |
| Effect on family property | No change | For a member of an undivided Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain family, Sections 19 and 21 (severance from the joint family, and succession under the Indian Succession Act) can apply, but Section 21A switches them off when both partners are Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh or Jain |

The Special Marriage Act figures are from the Act's text on India Code; under Section 13, its certificate is "conclusive evidence" of the marriage.

### Which route should you take?

- **Both Hindu/Sikh/Jain/Buddhist, wedding already done:** Hindu Marriage Act. It is quicker because there is no notice period.
- **Different religions:** Special Marriage Act, either from scratch (Section 5) or to register a wedding you have already had in another form (Sections 15 and 16, which also have a 30-day objection period).
- **Muslim, Christian or Parsi wedding:** These follow their own laws and documents (such as a nikahnama), and compulsory-registration laws like Delhi's 2014 Order cover them. In July 2024 the Delhi High Court told the government to add that Order to its online portal, *ThePrint* reported.

## Documents you will usually need

The New Delhi District Magistrate's list is a good, typical example. Your state may ask for slightly different papers:

1. Identity proof for both of you and for every witness (Aadhaar, PAN, passport, voter ID or driving licence)
2. Address proof for both of you, and for the witnesses
3. Date-of-birth proof for both of you
4. A certificate from the priest, granthi or other person who performed the wedding (for registering a ceremony that has already happened), and wedding photos or the invitation card where asked
5. An affidavit in the state's format declaring age, marital status and that you are not within prohibited relationships (Delhi asks for it on ₹10 e-stamp paper, notarised)
6. Divorce decree or the former spouse's death certificate, if either of you was married before
7. A certificate from the embassy, if one partner is a foreign national
8. Passport-size photographs

In Delhi and most states, both partners and the witnesses appear in person on the appointment day, with originals of everything uploaded.

## Step by step: how registration usually works

1. **Create an account on your state portal** and choose the right Act. Choosing the wrong Act can get the application rejected.
2. **Fill in details** for both partners and the witnesses, and upload the documents.
3. **Special Marriage Act only:** the notice is entered in the Marriage Notice Book and put up in the office. Wait 30 days. If someone objects, the officer must decide within 30 days, and you can appeal to the district court within 30 days if it goes against you.
4. **Book an appointment** and go together with your witnesses and the original documents.
5. **Sign the register.** Under the Special Marriage Act, both of you and the three witnesses sign a declaration before the officer.
6. **Collect or download the certificate** and check every name and date before you leave.

If an application is stuck with no reason given, an [RTI application](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-file-rti-online) asking for its status can help.

## Why the certificate matters for passports and visas

**Indian passport.** You don't need a marriage certificate to add a spouse's name to an Indian passport: since the passport rule changes the Ministry of External Affairs announced in December 2016, a joint declaration with a photograph of the couple (Annexure J) can be used instead. Still, the certificate remains the simplest proof, especially for a surname change.

**Foreign visas.** Spouse and family visas abroad need proof of the marriage, and a government certificate carries the most weight. The US State Department's India page says certificates come from the Registrar of Marriages in each district headquarters. It also says a "marriage agreement" or "deed of marriage" is *not* enough proof. Read its [India reciprocity page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/Visa-Reciprocity-and-Civil-Documents-by-Country/India.html) if you are applying for a US visa.

**Apostille.** For countries in the Hague Apostille Convention, the certificate is first authenticated by the state government and then apostilled by the Ministry of External Affairs. The MEA's apostille page lists marriage certificates among the personal documents it apostilles, for a fee of ₹50 per document, plus the outsourcing agency's service charges.

At home, it helps with nominations, insurance and pension claims, and joint property purchases, where the [stamp duty and registration charges](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/stamp-duty-and-registration-charges) are paid at the same sub-registrar offices that often register marriages.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the Special Marriage Act process take?

At least 30 days after the notice is published, because that is the objection period in Section 7. The marriage must then be solemnised within three calendar months of the notice (Section 14), or you have to give a fresh notice.

### Can we register a Hindu marriage years after the wedding?

Yes. Registration under the Hindu Marriage Act can be done later, though states with compulsory registration laws may charge a late fee. You will need proof of the ceremony, such as the priest's certificate and photographs, plus the usual affidavit and witnesses.

### Do inter-faith couples have to convert to marry?

No. The Special Marriage Act lets two people of different religions marry without changing religion, as long as they meet its age, consent and relationship conditions.

### Is a temple or Arya Samaj certificate enough?

It is evidence that a ceremony took place, but it is not a government registration certificate. Use it to register the marriage with the registrar, because passport offices and embassies look for the government-issued certificate.
