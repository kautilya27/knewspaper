**Quick answer:** Nonverbal communication is everything you "say" without words: facial expressions, tone of voice, gestures, posture, eye contact, touch, personal space and even silence. It matters most when people are judging how you feel, and it is heavily shaped by culture, so the same head movement or gaze can mean different things in Chennai, Chandigarh and Chicago.

Two things are worth knowing before you read any body-language advice. The famous claim that "93% of communication is nonverbal" comes from a misreading of two small 1967 studies. And research does not support the idea that you can spot a liar from their eyes or fidgeting.

## The 7-38-55 rule: what Mehrabian actually found

The claim that a message is 7% words, 38% tone and 55% body language is credited to psychologist Albert Mehrabian. It comes from two 1967 experiments:

- **Mehrabian and Wiener (1967)**, in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, played single words such as "dear" or "terrible" spoken in a positive, neutral or negative tone. Listeners judged the speaker's attitude more from the tone than from the word.
- **Mehrabian and Ferris (1967)**, in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology*, paired black-and-white photos of faces with recorded tones of voice. The face counted for more than the voice.

Combining the two gave the 7-38-55 split. But, as a [Donders Institute explainer](https://blog.donders.ru.nl/this-is-why-93-of-communication-is-nonverbal-is-a-myth/?lang=en) points out, the studies only looked at how people read **feelings and attitudes**, using single words, mostly when the word and the tone or face contradicted each other. Gestures and posture were not tested at all. The participants were also only women, a point Mehrabian's own account of the studies confirms.

Mehrabian himself has said, on his own website, that "unless a communicator is discussing their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable." So the honest takeaway is narrower: **when someone's words and tone clash ("I'm fine" said through gritted teeth), people tend to believe the tone and the face.**

## Types of nonverbal communication

Communication textbooks group nonverbal cues into these channels, each with an Indian example of how meanings can trip people up.

| Type | What it includes | Example to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Facial expressions | Smiles, frowns, raised eyebrows | A polite smile may mean "I don't fully agree" in a meeting where open disagreement feels rude |
| Paralanguage (voice) | Tone, pitch, speed, pauses, volume | A loud, fast voice reads as excitement in one family and anger in another |
| Gestures | Hand and head movements | The side-to-side head tilt, or pointing with one finger |
| Posture and body movement | How you sit, stand, lean | Crossed legs with the sole of the foot towards an elder can be seen as rude |
| Eye contact (oculesics) | How long and how directly you look | Lowered eyes with a senior can be respect, not low confidence |
| Touch (haptics) | Handshakes, pats, hugs | Touching an elder's feet; avoiding touch across genders |
| Personal space (proxemics) | Distance kept from others | Crowded queues and trains versus a client who steps back |
| Appearance and objects | Clothes, grooming, accessories | Formal vs ethnic wear at an interview or office function |
| Time (chronemics) | Punctuality, waiting, pace | What "10 o'clock" means for a meeting vs a family visit |
| Silence | Not replying, pauses | Silence after a proposal may signal disagreement, not acceptance |

## Indian nonverbal cues that confuse people

India is not one culture, and no gesture is used the same way by every Indian. With that caution, a few cues are well documented and often misread by colleagues from other countries, and sometimes by Indians from other regions.

### The head wobble (or tilt)

The side-to-side tilt of the head is the cue foreigners ask about most. Depending on context it can mean yes, okay, I understand, thank you, or a soft "maybe" that avoids a flat no. It is seen across India and is often described as more frequent in the south. The [Cultural Atlas](https://theculturalatlas.org/indian-culture/indian-culture-communication), a cross-cultural reference guide, notes that Indians may tilt or shake the head side to side to show agreement and understanding.

With international teams, add a word ("Yes, done", "I'll check") instead of relying on the tilt alone; and if you are on the receiving end, ask a direct follow-up rather than assuming a yes.

### Namaste, handshakes and touching feet

According to the Cultural Atlas, namaste with palms pressed together (the anjali gesture) is a common greeting, while handshakes are normal in professional and urban settings, using the right hand. Some people avoid touching someone of the opposite gender, so a safe practice is to let a woman offer her hand first, or to greet with a namaste. Touching an elder's feet (charan sparsh) is a respectful gesture, and the elder may respond by placing a palm on your head in blessing. Showing the soles of your feet, or touching someone with your feet, is considered rude.

### Eye contact, pointing and indirectness

The same guide says direct eye contact is generally fine in India as long as you look away every so often, and that women may avert their gaze more, especially with men. Pointing a finger at someone can feel accusatory; gesturing with the whole hand, palm down, is more polite.

Many Indians also keep disagreement indirect. As the Cultural Atlas puts it, "the absence of agreement may be an expression of disagreement." In a meeting, silence or a vague "we'll see" is not always a yes.

### Personal space

Edward Hall's well-known four "zones" of personal distance (intimate, personal, social, public) were based on observations of Americans, and Hall himself warned that cultures draw these lines differently. A large 2017 study in the *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology* by Sorokowska and colleagues asked 8,943 people in 42 countries, India among them, how close a stranger could stand before they felt uncomfortable. Preferred distance from a stranger ranged from about 76 cm in Argentina to about 140 cm in Romania, and women generally wanted more space. A colleague who steps back may simply have a different comfort zone.

## What research does not support

### "You can tell when someone is lying"

A 2006 review by Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo in *Personality and Social Psychology Review* pooled 206 documents with 24,483 judges. People sorted lies from truths correctly only 54% of the time, barely better than a coin toss. An earlier review by DePaulo and colleagues (2003) combined estimates for 158 possible cues from 116 reports. Most showed no link or only a weak link to lying; the few that did, such as liars seeming more tense or speaking at a slightly higher pitch, were far too weak to catch an individual liar. A nervous candidate may look away and fidget while telling the truth.

### "Power poses change your hormones"

A 2010 study claimed that standing in an expansive "power pose" for two minutes raised testosterone and lowered cortisol. When economists at the University of Zurich (Ranehill and colleagues, 2015) repeated it with 200 people, the poses made participants *feel* more powerful but changed neither their hormones nor their risk-taking. In 2016, Dana Carney, one of the original study's authors, wrote that she does not believe power pose effects are real. A 2022 meta-analysis of 128 studies by Körner and colleagues found a reliable effect on how people feel but none on hormones, and said the effect on behaviour remains unclear. Standing tall may help you feel steadier before an interview (see [how to build self-confidence](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-build-self-confidence) for approaches that last); it will not change your biology.

The same caution applies to "crossed arms mean defensive": no single gesture has one fixed meaning. Crossed arms can just mean a cold AC.

## Nonverbal communication in interviews and video calls

In an interview, the goal is not to "perform" body language but to remove signals that distract from your answers.

- **Greeting:** a right-hand handshake or a namaste both work in Indian offices; follow the interviewer's lead.
- **Eye contact:** look at whoever asked the question, and spread your gaze across a panel.
- **Voice:** nerves speed people up, so slow down for key points. Rehearsing your answer to ["Tell me about yourself"](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/tell-me-about-yourself) helps.
- **Mismatch:** "I'm very excited about this role" said in a flat voice is exactly the clash Mehrabian studied; the interviewer will believe the voice.

Video calls add their own strain. Stanford researcher Jeremy Bailenson argued in *Technology, Mind, and Behavior* (2021) that they cause "nonverbal overload": faces look unnaturally close, we keep watching ourselves, we can't move, and we must exaggerate nods for them to register. Fixes that follow:

- Keep the camera at eye level and look into it when you speak.
- Hide self-view once your framing and lighting are set.
- Nod clearly and say "yes" or "right" aloud, since small cues get lost on a laggy connection.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the main types of nonverbal communication?

The main types are facial expressions, tone of voice (paralanguage), gestures, posture, eye contact, touch, personal space, appearance, use of time and silence. Each is read through the lens of culture and context.

### Is it true that 93% of communication is nonverbal?

No. The 7-38-55 figures come from two small 1967 studies about judging feelings from single words, tones and facial photos. Albert Mehrabian himself says the numbers don't apply unless someone is talking about feelings or attitudes.

### What does the Indian head wobble mean?

It usually signals yes, okay or "I understand", and can also mean thank you or a polite maybe. Its meaning depends on context, so in workplaces with international colleagues it helps to add a spoken word.

### Can body language tell you if someone is lying?

Not reliably. A review pooling 206 research documents found people spot lies correctly only about 54% of the time, and no single nonverbal cue, including looking away, consistently gives a liar away.
