**Quick answer:** India has **eight** classical dances recognised by the Sangeet Natak Akademi (SNA), the national academy for music, dance and drama: Bharatanatyam (Tamil Nadu), Kathak (north India), Kathakali (Kerala), Kuchipudi (Andhra Pradesh), Odissi (Odisha), Manipuri (Manipur), Mohiniyattam (Kerala) and Sattriya (Assam).

You will also see the figure **nine**. That comes from the Ministry of Culture, whose dance page also lists **Chhau** of Odisha, Jharkhand and West Bengal. Below we explain where each count comes from, then give a table of the visual and musical clues that help you tell the forms apart on stage.

## Why some lists say 8 and others say 9

No law or gazette notification sets the number of classical dances. "Classical" is a label that grew out of scholarship and government cultural bodies. The two counts come from different places.

**The Sangeet Natak Akademi's eight.** This is the list most exam guides use. The last form added was Sattriya. According to the dance portal Narthaki, SNA secretary Jayant Kastuar announced on **15 November 2000**, at a Sattriya performance in Guwahati, that the Akademi recognised it as a classical dance. A Deccan Herald feature describes how it happened: a four-day conference held by the Akademi in Guwahati, with Assamese gurus and Sattriya exponents, agreed unanimously that the form had the essential features of a classical dance. Bhupen Hazarika, then SNA chairman, played a key part.

**The Ministry of Culture's nine.** The [Ministry of Culture's dance page](https://www.indiaculture.gov.in/dance) describes nine forms, ending with Chhau. But the same page calls Chhau "a blend of martial traditions, temple rituals, and folk and popular performance", which is not how it describes the other eight. In practice, Chhau sits on the ministry's list of major dance traditions without being formally ranked with them.

**Scheme lists go wider still.** The Scholarship to Young Artistes scheme guidelines (run through the Centre for Cultural Resources and Training, CCRT) put "Thang-ta, Gaudiya Nritya, Chhau Dance/Music, Sattriya Dance" under the heading "Indian Classical Dance / Dance Music", next to the eight. The ministry's 2024 list of empanelled artists has a "Classical & Traditional Dance form" category that also includes Yakshagana and Chhau. These lists decide who can apply for funding. They don't declare a dance classical.

**Other claims you may read about:**

- **Gaudiya Nritya** (West Bengal) is presented as a classical form by its practitioners, but it has not been added to the SNA list. Dance writers have questioned how its repertoire was rebuilt.
- **Chhau** has supporters who argue the Seraikella and Mayurbhanj styles deserve classical status. An Asian Age column in 2018 said the idea had come up before but went nowhere.
- **Yakshagana and Bhagavata Mela** appear in some scholars' lists of classical dance-theatre, not in the SNA count.

We found no official announcement of a ninth SNA classical form up to 28 November 2026. For exams, write eight (SNA), and add that the Ministry of Culture also lists Chhau.

## How to tell them apart: what to look and listen for

Most lists stop at the state. This table uses the features that you can actually see or hear in a performance, drawn mainly from the Ministry of Culture's dance page and NCERT's textbook chapter on dance.

| Dance | Costume and make-up | Stance and footwork | Hands and face | Giveaway sign |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bharatanatyam | Silk sari stitched with a fan of pleats in front, temple jewellery | Deep half-sit (aramandi), crisp, geometric lines | Sharp, clearly held mudras | Knees bent outward, fan pleats opening in each squat |
| Kathak | Anarkali or lehenga with dupatta | Upright stance (sama sthana), rhythmic stamping, ankle bells | Gestures close to everyday body language | Fast spins (chakkars) and stamping that rings the ankle bells |
| Kathakali | Huge headdress, billowing skirt, painted face: green for heroes and gods, red and black for villains | Very deep, wide stance | Large vocabulary of mudras and eye movement; the actor never speaks | Mask-like painted face and towering crown |
| Kuchipudi | Silk costume, gochi kattu drape, temple jewellery | Like aramandi but less deep, quick and bouncy | Mudras plus spoken lines by the dancer | Dancing on the rim of a brass plate |
| Odissi | Pattasari silk with fan pleats, silver filigree jewellery, flower-like headpiece (tahiya) | Square half-sit (chauka) and tribhanga, the body bent at three points | Over fifty hastas, poses like temple sculptures | S-shaped body curve and silver ornaments |
| Manipuri | Women in a stiff, decorated barrel-like skirt (potloi) and veil; men in white dhoti and turban | Soft feet, knees close, footwork never heard | Subtle mudras, calm face, no eye contact with audience | Gliding, circular movements; men dancing while playing the pung drum |
| Mohiniyattam | White or cream sari with gold border (kasavu) | Gentle, swaying, sliding steps; no heavy stamping | Soft hand movements and gentle expression | All-white-and-gold look and slow sway of the upper body |
| Sattriya | Assamese pat silk with dhoti or skirt | Separate male (purusha) and female (prakriti) styles; training of 64 exercises (mati-akhora) | Upper body expresses, lower body keeps rhythm | Drummers and cymbal players (Gayan-Bayan) who may open group pieces |
| Chhau | Masks in the Seraikella and Purulia styles (not in Mayurbhanj) | Leaps, martial moves, vigorous turns | Expression through the whole body | Masks and drums at a night-time open-air show |

The costume is often your fastest clue. A white-and-gold Kerala sari points to Mohiniyattam; Assamese pat silk to Sattriya; a stiff barrel skirt to Manipuri. ### Two pairs people often confuse

- **Bharatanatyam vs Kuchipudi.** Both use Carnatic music and similar costumes. Kuchipudi is faster and more playful, the dancer may speak or sing lines, and a brass-plate section gives it away. Bharatanatyam holds sharper, more angular poses.
- **Kathakali vs Mohiniyattam.** Both are from Kerala, and that's where the likeness ends. Kathakali is a loud, painted dance-drama that traditionally ran from evening until daybreak. Mohiniyattam is a soft solo in white and gold, named after Mohini, the enchantress form of Vishnu.

## Where origins are debated

Most of these forms have long histories, but exact dates are often uncertain. Tread carefully with any single "founding year" you read online.

- **Sattriya** grew in the sattras (Vaishnava monasteries) founded by Srimanta Sankardeva. Sources differ on whether to say the 15th or 16th century, because Sankardeva's life spans both.
- **Bharatanatyam** in its modern recital form was shaped by the Tanjore Quartet in the 19th century, according to the Ministry of Culture. It grew from the older dance called Sadir or Dasi Attam.
- **Kathakali** took shape around the 17th century, according to the ministry, from older Kerala traditions.
- **Odissi** was rebuilt for the stage in the mid-20th century from the dances of temple servants (maharis) and other traditions.

Guides often link these dances to the Natya Shastra, the ancient Sanskrit text on performance. The link is about shared grammar (mudras, rasas), not proof that each modern form is unchanged since ancient times.

Chhau, by contrast, has a firm international marker: UNESCO added it to its [Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/chhau-dance-00337) in 2010, naming its three styles, Seraikella, Purulia and Mayurbhanj.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many classical dances are there in India?

The Sangeet Natak Akademi recognises eight: Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam and Sattriya. The Ministry of Culture's dance page lists nine because it adds Chhau.

### Which was the last dance to get classical status?

Sattriya of Assam. According to Narthaki, the Sangeet Natak Akademi announced its recognition on 15 November 2000 in Guwahati.

### Is Chhau a classical dance?

It is not on the Sangeet Natak Akademi's list of eight. The Ministry of Culture includes it among India's major dances but describes it as a blend of martial, ritual and folk traditions, so exam answers usually treat it as the "ninth" form only when they follow the ministry.

### Which states have two classical dances?

On the Sangeet Natak Akademi's list, Kerala has two, Kathakali and Mohiniyattam. On the Ministry of Culture's list of nine, Odisha also has two: Odissi and Chhau. Every other form is linked to one state or region, while Chhau is shared by Odisha, Jharkhand and West Bengal.
