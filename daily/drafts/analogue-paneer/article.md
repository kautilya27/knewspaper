**Quick answer:** Analogue paneer is a paneer look-alike in which some or all of the milk ingredients are replaced by non-milk ones, usually vegetable fat or oil, starch and emulsifiers. Under FSSAI rules it is not paneer, it is not even a milk product, and it must not be sold or served as paneer. Real paneer has to come from milk, curdled with a permitted acid and heated.

Selling it honestly labelled has been legal at the national level so far. But in 2026 at least seven states, including Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and Telangana, banned or restricted it, most of them outright, and FSSAI has published a draft rule that would stop any analogue product from using the word "paneer" at all. No home trick can reliably tell you whether paneer contains vegetable fat. Only a lab test can.

## What the law counts as paneer

FSSAI's standards for dairy products, the Food Safety and Standards (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, 2011, set out the paneer standard in regulation 2.1.16. It defines chhana or paneer as the product obtained from milk, with or without added milk solids, by precipitation with permitted acidulants and heating. The permitted acidulants are lactic acid, citric acid, malic acid, vinegar, glucono delta lactone and sour whey.

The standard sets limits on both moisture and milk fat:

| Type | Moisture (maximum) | Milk fat, on dry matter |
|---|---|---|
| Paneer | 60% | at least 50% |
| Medium-fat paneer | 60% | more than 20% but less than 50% |
| Low-fat paneer | 70% | not more than 20% |

The fat must be **milk fat**, so "paneer" made with palm oil or vanaspati fails the standard however creamy it tastes. Medium-fat and low-fat paneer may be sold only in sealed packs.

## What makes a product "analogue"

The same regulations (sub-regulation 2.1.1) define an analogue in the dairy context as a product in which constituents not derived from milk take the place, in part or in whole, of milk constituents, and which looks, tastes or works like a milk product. The rules then say plainly that analogues "are not considered milk, milk products or composite milk products".

The current labelling rules, as summarised in FSSAI's April 2025 consultation paper:

- If a non-milk ingredient replaces part of the milk, the label must say "Contains ..." naming that ingredient and its source (for example, vegetable fat from palm oil), close to the product name.
- If a milk ingredient is replaced completely, it must say "Contains no milk ..." for that ingredient.
- If the product is meant to substitute a milk product, the label and advertising cannot use dairy terms or suggest it is a milk product.
- The FSS (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020 separately ban any label that is false, misleading or likely to create a wrong impression.

## The main 2025–2026 orders, in date order

**April 2025: FSSAI consultation paper.** FSSAI proposed a "Non-dairy" or "Analogue" prefix before dairy words, menu disclosure in restaurants (it gave kadhai paneer as an example) and sale only in packs of at least 500 g. These were proposals, not law.

**April 2026: FSSAI West Region directions.** Public Notice (Enforcement) 3/2026, dated 21 April 2026, called selling analogue as paneer "a grave violation of the law". It was about cheese analogues and was aimed at centrally licensed manufacturers and food businesses, including restaurants. It told them to train buyers and chefs, test incoming raw material, keep supplier invoices and disclose any analogue use on menus or display boards.

**30 July 2026: Maharashtra bans it.** The Commissioner of Food Safety, Maharashtra, banned the manufacture, storage, transport, sale and supply of analogue or non-dairy paneer "under any name" across the state for one year, using Section 30(2)(a) of the FSS Act. The order says no hotel, restaurant, caterer, eatery or cloud kitchen may use it. **Menu disclosure does not make it legal there.** In August 2026, according to LiveLaw, the Bombay High Court refused interim relief to a restaurant whose licence was suspended over analogue paneer.

**About 1 August 2026: Chhattisgarh.** According to Patrika, the state banned the manufacture and sale of analogue paneer through a gazette notification, ordered around 18 factories to be sealed and their licences cancelled, and told food safety officers to check hotels, dhabas and markets.

**5 August 2026: Gujarat.** According to the order as reported by TeamLease RegTech and Down To Earth, the Food and Drugs Control Administration, Gujarat, prohibited non-standardised analogues imitating paneer, cream or butter, including those using vegetable fat in place of milk fat. Restaurants and caterers must use only genuine dairy. Frozen dessert, processed cheese and mixed fat spread, which have their own standards, are excluded. The ban runs for one year or until FSSAI notifies final standards.

**12 August 2026: Madhya Pradesh.** According to the Free Press Journal, the state's Food Commissioner issued an order with immediate effect, following a directive from the Chief Minister. It bans making, processing, storing, transporting, distributing and selling analogue or non-dairy products that imitate paneer, cream, butter, mawa or other dairy items. Seizures followed in Indore.

**17 August 2026: Karnataka.** According to The South First, the state's food safety department banned analogue or non-dairy paneer sold under the name "paneer" for one year, and barred its use in any food product. Frozen dessert, processed cheese and mixed fat spread are excluded.

**20 August 2026: Himachal Pradesh.** According to GKToday, the Commissioner of Food Safety banned the making, storage, distribution and sale of analogue paneer as "paneer" for one year under Section 30(2)(a); any non-dairy substitute used in food must be declared to customers.

**10 September 2026: Telangana.** According to The Daily Pioneer, the state's food safety authority imposed a one-year ban under Section 30(2)(a) of the FSS Act.

**23 September 2026: FSSAI's national draft.** The draft Food Safety and Standards (Prohibition and Restrictions on Sales) Amendment Regulations, 2026 would add "paneer made of constituents not derived from milk" to the list of banned items. Products licensed as "Analogue in Dairy Context" would have to stop using the word "paneer" in their names, labels and marketing. The 60-day comment window closed in November 2026; until FSSAI notifies the final rule, it remains a draft. You can read it on [FSSAI's website](https://www.fssai.gov.in/docs/food-law/notice-comment/Notification%20dated%2023.09.2026.pdf).

Outside these states, the national rules still apply: a restaurant serving analogue as "paneer tikka" is misleading you.

## Home tests: which ones actually work

Most viral tests check for one thing and then get used for another.

| Test | What it really shows | Can it catch vegetable fat? |
|---|---|---|
| Iodine (tincture) drops turn the paneer blue-black | Starch is present | **No** |
| Crumbling, rubbery texture, or water leaking when pressed | Texture only. Fresh, low-fat or poorly pressed real paneer can also behave oddly | No |
| Paneer goes stretchy or melts in a hot pan | Hints at added fat or emulsifiers, but cooking and moisture vary | Not reliably |
| Smell, taste and "milky" flavour | Personal judgement. Flavours can be added | No |
| Very low price | A reason to ask questions, not proof | No |

Iodine reacts with starch, and real paneer contains none, so a blue-black colour is a genuine red flag. But many analogues are mostly vegetable fat with little or no starch, and these pass the iodine test cleanly. A paneer that stays brown is not proven genuine.

**What only a lab can tell you.** To find out whether the fat in paneer is milk fat, the fat has to be extracted and tested. FSSAI's manual of dairy testing methods includes the Butyro-refractometer reading, the Reichert-Meissl and Polenske values, the Baudouin test for vanaspati, fatty acid profiling by gas-liquid chromatography, and RP-HPLC to detect vegetable oil in milk fat. A lab also measures moisture and fat on dry matter against the limits in the table above. None of this can be done in a kitchen.

## What you can do instead

- **Read the pack.** Look for "Contains vegetable fat", "Contains no milk fat" or "Analogue" near the name. Real paneer lists only milk (or milk solids) and an acidulant.
- **Check the licence.** Packed food must carry an FSSAI licence number, which you can look up on FSSAI's FoSCoS portal. Selling paneer dishes from home? See our guide to [FSSAI registration for a home food business](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/fssai-registration-for-home-food-business).
- **Ask at restaurants.** Under FSSAI's directions, a restaurant should know whether its paneer is dairy and hold supplier invoices. In states with a ban (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Telangana and Chhattisgarh) it should not be using analogue at all.
- **Complain.** Report suspected adulteration through FSSAI's Food Safety Connect app or your state food safety department, which can take an official sample for lab testing.

## Is analogue paneer harmful?

Honestly labelled analogue from a licensed maker is not illegal nationally. The bigger problems are deception and nutrition: you pay for milk protein and milk fat and get vegetable fat and starch. If you eat paneer mainly for protein, see our list of [the best protein sources](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/best-protein-sources). Poor quality is a separate worry. According to Moneylife, Maharashtra's tests found 109 of 308 paneer and dairy-analogue samples taken between April 2025 and March 2026 failed, 79 as sub-standard and 30 as unsafe.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is analogue paneer banned in India?

Not yet nationally. At least seven states have banned or restricted it in 2026, among them Maharashtra (one year from July 2026), Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Himachal Pradesh and Telangana, and FSSAI's September 2026 draft would stop any analogue from being sold as "paneer". Everywhere, an analogue must not be labelled or served as paneer.

### Does the iodine test detect analogue paneer?

Only if the analogue contains starch. Iodine turns blue-black with starch but does not react to vegetable fat. A paneer that passes the iodine test can still be made with palm oil.

### Is tofu the same as analogue paneer?

No. Tofu is a soybean product with its own FSSAI standard and is sold under its own name, so FSSAI does not treat it as a dairy analogue. Analogue paneer is a product designed to imitate paneer.

### How can I get paneer tested?

Ask your state food safety department to draw a sample, or send it to an FSSAI-notified food testing laboratory. The lab can check moisture, milk fat on dry matter and the fat's profile to see whether vegetable fat has been used.
