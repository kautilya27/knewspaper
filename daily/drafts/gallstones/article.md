**Quick answer:** Gallstones are hard lumps that form in the gallbladder when bile, the digestive fluid stored there, has too much cholesterol or bilirubin, or when the gallbladder doesn't empty properly. Most people who have them feel nothing at all. When a stone blocks the outlet, it causes sudden, steady pain in the upper middle or upper right tummy for half an hour to several hours, often after a heavy meal or at night.

Pain with fever, shivering, yellow eyes or skin, or vomiting with pain spreading to the back is an emergency: go to a hospital. In India, gallstones are far more common in the north and east than in the south, and doctors in those regions weigh "silent" stones a little differently because of the higher rate of gallbladder cancer there.

## What gallstones are and why they form

The gallbladder is a small pouch under the liver that stores bile and squeezes it into the gut when you eat. According to the US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), stones form when:

- bile carries more cholesterol than the bile salts can dissolve,
- bile has too much bilirubin, a pigment made when red blood cells break down, or
- the gallbladder doesn't empty fully or often enough, so bile sits and thickens.

**Cholesterol stones** (yellow-green) are the most common type; **pigment stones** are dark and made of bilirubin; some stones are mixed.

India shows a regional split. Studies summarised by gastroenterologist R.K. Tandon in the *World Journal of Gastroenterology* found mostly cholesterol stones in north Indian patients and mostly pigment stones in south Indian patients. Diet or genes may explain it; nobody knows for sure.

## Symptoms of gallstones

Most gallstones are "silent". An NHS England decision aid for patients says about 20 in every 100 people with gallstones get painful symptoms, and 80 do not. Many people find out only when a scan for something else shows them.

When symptoms do come, the typical pattern is **biliary colic**:

- Pain in the upper middle of the tummy or just under the right ribs, lasting from about 30 minutes to several hours (NHS).
- It may spread to the right shoulder or between the shoulder blades, says Cleveland Clinic.
- Nausea or vomiting.
- It often starts after a large or fatty meal, and often in the evening or at night (NIDDK).

The pain usually settles once the stone moves, but NIDDK notes that after one attack, more are likely. Vague bloating or acidity on its own is not the typical pattern and has many other causes.

### Emergency signs: go to a hospital

Based on NHS and NIDDK guidance, get urgent medical care if you have:

- sudden, severe tummy pain, or pain that lasts several hours,
- pain spreading to the back, with vomiting,
- fever, chills or shivering,
- yellow skin or yellow whites of the eyes (jaundice),
- tea-coloured urine or pale, clay-coloured stools.

These can mean an inflamed gallbladder (cholecystitis), an infected bile duct (cholangitis) or an inflamed pancreas (pancreatitis), all of which need hospital care. Don't drive yourself. For tummy pain lasting over 30 minutes without these signs, the NHS still advises seeing a doctor urgently.

## Who gets gallstones: the main causes and risk factors

The NHS and NIDDK list these as the main risk factors:

- **Being a woman.** Oestrogen raises the cholesterol in bile. Pregnancy, birth control pills and hormone replacement therapy can add to the risk.
- **Age** over 40 (NHS).
- **Obesity**, especially in women (see our guide to [belly fat](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/belly-fat)).
- **Rapid weight loss**, including after weight-loss surgery or very low-calorie crash diets.
- **Diet** high in calories and refined carbohydrates and low in fibre (here are some everyday [high-fibre foods](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/high-fibre-foods)).
- **Diabetes**, insulin resistance and high triglycerides.
- **Family history** of gallstones.
- **Illnesses** such as cirrhosis, Crohn's disease and haemolytic anaemias like sickle cell disease.

## How common are gallstones in India?

India has no single national figure. Community studies show:

| Study | Where | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| Tandon, *World J Gastroenterol* (2000) | Delhi, 1,104 people screened by ultrasound | 4.3% had gallstones. 7.4% of Punjabis, 4.4% of Bengalis, 1.8% of south Indians in the sample |
| Same review, citing a 1966 railway workers' survey | North vs south India | Gallstones about 7 times more common in north Indian workers |
| Unisa et al., *HPB* (2011) | Rural Patna, Vaishali and Varanasi | 4.15% overall. 5.59% of women vs 1.99% of men |
| Dutta et al., *Chinese Clinical Oncology* (2019) | Review of Indian data | Symptomatic gallstones about 20 times more common in north India than south India (the review gives no primary source for this) |

The pattern is the same in every study: more common in the north and east, and, where measured, two to three times more common in women.

## Gallstones and gallbladder cancer in the Gangetic belt

India has some of the world's highest rates of gallbladder cancer, concentrated in one region. A 2019 review by Indian gastroenterologists and surgeons ([Dutta et al., *Chinese Clinical Oncology*](https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/28518/25067)) used Indian cancer registry data. It found age-standardised rates of about 11.8 per lakh women in north India and 17.1 per lakh women in the north-east, compared with less than 1 per lakh in much of south and west India. The high-risk belt follows the Ganga, Yamuna, Sutlej and Brahmaputra rivers, covering states such as Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Assam, Punjab and Haryana.

According to the same review, about 80% of Indian patients with gallbladder cancer also have gallstones. Gallstones are a major risk factor, but not the only one. In the Gangetic-basin study by Unisa and colleagues, gallbladder disease was more common where people drank water from wells, ponds or rivers, and in villages whose water had more nickel, cadmium and chromium; whether such factors also raise cancer risk is still being studied.

Keep it in proportion. Even in the highest-risk registries, gallbladder cancer affects roughly 12 to 17 women per lakh per year. Meanwhile, about 4 to 6 in every 100 adults in the region have gallstones. So the vast majority of people with gallstones never get this cancer. It is a reason to discuss a scan report with your doctor, not a reason to panic.

## How gallstones are diagnosed in India

An **abdominal ultrasound** is the standard first test. It is widely available, uses no radiation and finds most gallbladder stones. Blood tests (liver function tests, and sometimes amylase or lipase and a blood count) help check for jaundice, infection or pancreatitis.

If a stone may be stuck in the main bile duct, doctors may order an **MRCP**, a type of MRI scan.

## Silent stones vs painful stones: when surgery is advised

This is your doctor's call, but here is the general picture from the NHS:

- **No symptoms:** usually no treatment. You may be asked to come back for check-ups.
- **Symptoms, complications or stones in the bile duct:** the usual treatment is removing the gallbladder (cholecystectomy), mostly by keyhole surgery. Stones in the bile duct are usually cleared with ERCP, using a tube passed through the mouth.

India has no single national guideline on silent stones. Because of the regional cancer risk, the Dutta review says surgeons may lean towards removal even without symptoms in some cases: very large stones (over 3 cm), a non-working gallbladder, gallbladder polyps, a family history of gallbladder cancer, a long life expectancy (more than 20 years, so usually younger patients), or living far from good surgical care. Your surgeon weighs these against your age and health.

You can live normally without a gallbladder, because the liver keeps making bile. The NHS England decision aid notes that about 10 in 100 people have looser stools afterwards.

### Gallbladder flushes and home remedies

Mayo Clinic says there is no reliable evidence that a gallbladder "cleanse" with oil and juice prevents or treats gallstones. The "stones" people see afterwards are clumps of oil and juice. Cleanses can also cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea.

## Gallstones vs kidney stones

They are different problems in different organs. See our guide to [kidney stone symptoms and causes](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/kidney-stones-symptoms-causes).

| | Gallstones | Kidney stones |
|---|---|---|
| Organ | Gallbladder (digestive system) | Kidneys and urinary tract |
| Where it hurts | Upper middle or upper right tummy, may spread to right shoulder or back | Side or lower back, may spread to lower tummy and groin |
| Warning signs | Jaundice, pale stools, dark urine, fever | Blood in urine, burning or frequent urination, fever |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can gallstones go away on their own?

Don't count on it. An attack often settles when a stone shifts, but more attacks are likely afterwards. Silent stones often need no treatment, so ask your doctor about yours.

### Does eating oily food cause gallstones?

A heavy, fatty meal can trigger an attack if you already have stones. The long-term risk is linked more to obesity and a high-calorie, low-fibre diet.

### Are gallstones common in young women?

They are two to three times more common in women than men, and pregnancy and oestrogen-containing pills can raise the risk. Upper-right tummy pain in pregnancy should always be checked by a doctor.

### Do all gallstones turn into cancer?

No. Most people with gallstones never develop gallbladder cancer, even in high-risk parts of north and north-east India. Very large stones, polyps or a non-working gallbladder raise the concern, so let a doctor review your scan.
