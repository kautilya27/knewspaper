**Quick answer:** The volume of a sphere is **V = (4/3)πr³**, where *r* is the radius. For a sphere of radius 7 cm, V = (4/3) × (22/7) × 7 × 7 × 7 = **1,437.33 cm³** (about 1437⅓ cm³). A hemisphere is half of that: **V = (2/3)πr³**.

The answer is always in cubic units (cm³, m³). If you're given the diameter, halve it first. Forgetting that step is the single most common way students lose marks on this formula.

## The formulas at a glance

| Shape | Volume | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sphere | (4/3)πr³ | r = radius |
| Sphere, using diameter d | (1/6)πd³ | Same formula, since r = d/2 |
| Hemisphere (solid) | (2/3)πr³ | Half a sphere |
| Hollow sphere (shell) | (4/3)π(R³ − r³) | R = outer radius, r = inner radius |
| Hollow hemisphere (bowl) | (2/3)π(R³ − r³) | Volume of the material |

Don't confuse these with the surface area formulas: a sphere's surface area is 4πr², and a solid hemisphere's total surface area is 3πr² (curved 2πr² plus the flat circle πr²). Area has r², volume has r³.

## Which value of π should you use?

- Use **22/7** when the radius is a multiple of 7 (7, 14, 21, 3.5, 10.5). NCERT questions are usually built this way so the 7s cancel.
- Use **3.14** when the question says so, or when the radius isn't a multiple of 7.
- If a question says **"leave your answer in terms of π"**, don't substitute at all. For r = 3 cm, V = (4/3)π × 27 = **36π cm³**.

Always use the value the question asks for. Using 3.14 where 22/7 was given changes the decimals and can cost you the mark in an objective question.

## Where the formula comes from (without calculus)

The full proof needs integration, which comes in Class 12. At school level, there are two neat ways to see why (4/3)πr³ is right.

### Archimedes' sphere and cylinder

Put a sphere of radius r snugly inside a cylinder of the same radius and height 2r. The Greek mathematician Archimedes showed, in his work *On the Sphere and Cylinder*, that the sphere takes up exactly **two-thirds** of the cylinder. He was so proud of this result that he asked for a sphere inside a cylinder to be carved on his tomb.

Check it: cylinder volume = πr² × 2r = 2πr³. Two-thirds of that is **(4/3)πr³**.

### The cone, hemisphere and cylinder activity

Take a cylinder, a cone and a hemisphere, all with radius r, with the cylinder and cone of height r.

- Cylinder: πr² × r = πr³
- Cone: (1/3)πr² × r = (1/3)πr³
- So the hemisphere must hold the rest: πr³ − (1/3)πr³ = **(2/3)πr³**

You can test this with water: a hemispherical bowl filled with water, poured into a cone of the same radius and height r, fills it twice. Doubling the hemisphere gives the sphere, (4/3)πr³.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Volume from the radius

Find the volume of a sphere of radius 3.5 cm.

V = (4/3) × (22/7) × 3.5³ = (4/3) × (22/7) × 42.875 = (4/3) × 134.75 ≈ **179.67 cm³**

### Example 2: Volume from the diameter

A ball has a diameter of 42 cm. Find its volume.

Radius = 42 ÷ 2 = 21 cm.
V = (4/3) × (22/7) × 21 × 21 × 21 = 4 × 22 × 21 × 21 = **38,808 cm³**

### Example 3: Radius from the volume (reverse question)

A sphere has a volume of 38,808 cm³. Find its radius.

(4/3) × (22/7) × r³ = 38,808
r³ = 38,808 × 3 × 7 ÷ (4 × 22) = 9,261
r = ∛9,261 = **21 cm**

Learn the cubes up to 21³ (at least 7³ = 343, 14³ = 2,744, 21³ = 9,261); they turn up again and again in these questions.

### Example 4: Hemispherical bowl in litres

How much water can a hemispherical bowl of inner radius 10.5 cm hold?

V = (2/3) × (22/7) × 10.5³ = (2/3) × (22/7) × 1,157.625 = 2,425.5 cm³
Since 1,000 cm³ = 1 litre, the bowl holds about **2.43 litres**.

### Example 5: Hollow sphere

A hollow metal ball has an outer radius of 7 cm and an inner radius of 3.5 cm. Find the volume of metal.

V = (4/3) × (22/7) × (7³ − 3.5³) = (4/3) × (22/7) × (343 − 42.875) = **1,257.67 cm³** (approx.)

### Example 6: What happens when the radius doubles?

Volume depends on r³, so doubling the radius multiplies the volume by 2³ = **8**. Tripling it multiplies the volume by 27. The same idea gives ratios: two spheres with radii in the ratio 1 : 2 have volumes in the ratio 1 : 8.

## Real-life examples

- **Cricket ball.** The MCC Laws of Cricket (Law 4) set the circumference of a men's new ball at 22.4–22.9 cm. Taking 22.9 cm: r = 22.9 ÷ 2π ≈ 3.64 cm, so V ≈ (4/3)π(3.64)³ ≈ **203 cm³**.
- **Football.** The IFAB Laws of the Game (Law 2) set a match ball's circumference at 68–70 cm. At 70 cm, r ≈ 11.14 cm and V ≈ **5,790 cm³**, about 5.8 litres measured from the outside.
- **The Earth.** NASA gives Earth's mean radius as 6,371 km. Treating it as a sphere, V ≈ (4/3)π(6,371)³ ≈ **1.08 × 10¹² km³**, which matches NASA's figure of 108.321 × 10¹⁰ km³.

## Common mistakes that cost marks

1. **Using the diameter as the radius.** If the question says "diameter 14 cm", r is 7 cm. Using 14 makes your answer eight times too big.
2. **Squaring instead of cubing.** r² is for area. Volume always has r³.
3. **Mixing up the hemisphere formulas.** Volume is (2/3)πr³. Total surface area is 3πr². They look similar on a crowded answer sheet.
4. **Wrong units.** Volume is in cm³ or m³, not cm². For capacity, convert: 1,000 cm³ = 1 litre and 1 m³ = 1,000 litres.
5. **Rounding too early.** Keep fractions until the last step; round once at the end.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the formula for the volume of a sphere?

V = (4/3)πr³, where r is the radius. If you know the diameter d, you can also use V = (1/6)πd³.

### What is the volume of a hemisphere?

A solid hemisphere's volume is (2/3)πr³, exactly half the volume of a sphere with the same radius.

### How do you find the radius if the volume is given?

Rearrange the formula: r³ = 3V ÷ 4π, then take the cube root. With π = 22/7, this often gives a whole number such as 7, 14 or 21.

### Why is there a 4/3 in the formula?

It comes from Archimedes' result that a sphere fills two-thirds of the cylinder around it. Two-thirds of the cylinder's volume, 2πr³, is (4/3)πr³.
