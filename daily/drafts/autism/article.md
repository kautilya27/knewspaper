**Quick answer:** Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a difference in how the brain develops. Autistic people find social communication harder in some way and often have repetitive behaviours, strong interests, a need for routine or unusual reactions to sound, light, touch and taste. Signs usually appear in the first two to three years, though many people are identified much later.

There is no single cause. The World Health Organization (WHO) says many genetic and environmental factors probably work together, and **vaccines do not cause autism**. If you are worried about a child, ask a paediatrician or the District Early Intervention Centre (DEIC) at your district hospital for an assessment.

## What autism is, and why it is called a spectrum

"Spectrum" means autistic people differ widely. WHO notes that intellectual ability ranges "from profound impairment to superior levels". Some speak fluently and live independently; others use few or no words and need daily support. Needs can change over time.

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act, 2016 describes ASD as "a neuro-developmental condition typically appearing in the first three years of life" that affects a person's ability to communicate, understand relationships and relate to others.

Many autistic adults see autism as part of who they are, and the NHS calls it something you are born with, "not an illness". Support still matters: the aim is to help the person communicate, learn and live well, not to "cure" them.

### How common is autism in India?

WHO estimates that about **1 in 127 people** worldwide were autistic in 2021. The best Indian data comes from an INCLEN Trust study published in *PLOS Medicine* in 2018, which assessed 3,964 children aged 2 to 9 in Palwal, Kangra, Dhenkanal, Hyderabad and North Goa. About **1 in 100 children aged 2–5** had autism (about 1.4% at ages 6–9), similar to global figures. The same study found 13.6% of children aged 6–9 had at least one neurodevelopmental condition, such as ADHD or a learning disability.

## Symptoms of autism spectrum disorder

Signs fall into three groups. No two autistic people have the same mix, and one or two of these traits alone don't mean a child is autistic.

- **Social communication:** speaking late or using only single words, not responding to their name, using eye contact, gestures and facial expressions less often, repeating words or phrases, talking a lot about their own interest without a back-and-forth.
- **Behaviour and play:** little interest in playing with other children, no pretend play, repetitive actions (such as opening and closing doors or lining up toys), repeated movements like rocking, spinning or hand-flapping (often called stimming), getting very upset by changes in routine.
- **Sensory differences:** reacting very strongly, or not at all, to noise, light, smells, tastes or textures; disliking crowded or noisy places; eating only a few foods or refusing certain clothes.

### Early signs by age

Based on signs listed by the NHS and the Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP). Use it to know what to mention to a doctor, not as a diagnostic checklist.

| Age | Signs parents and teachers sometimes notice |
|---|---|
| Babies (under 18 months) | Few warm, joyful expressions shared with you; little back-and-forth babbling; an unusually "quiet, undemanding" baby; responding to their name only sometimes, so parents wonder about hearing |
| Toddlers (18 months to 3 years) | Speech late or not growing; few gestures; not joining other children; no pretend play; repetitive play; **losing words or social skills they already had** |
| School age | Few close friends; taking words literally or missing sarcasm; very strong interests; distress when rules or routines change; trouble with changes like a new school |
| Teenagers | Spending more time alone; rituals that get in the way of daily life; masking (copying others to fit in) |

The IAP's 2017 consensus statement notes that a quarter to a third of autistic children reach early language milestones and then lose language, communication or social skills between 15 and 24 months. **Any loss of speech or social skills, at any age, needs a doctor's visit soon.**

### Autism in girls

The NHS says autism can be harder to spot in girls, who may copy how other children play and seem to cope socially. Autistic girls may also be more likely to have anxiety, depression or an eating disorder.

### Conditions that often occur with autism

WHO lists epilepsy, depression, anxiety and ADHD as common in autistic people. If attention is also a concern, read [how ADHD is defined](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/add-vs-adhd); for teenagers and adults, the [signs of anxiety](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/anxiety-symptoms-and-causes) are worth knowing.

## What causes autism?

Research points to genes and to some factors before or around birth. These raise the *likelihood* of autism; most children exposed to them are not autistic.

| Factor | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Genes and family | Having an autistic brother or sister raises the likelihood (US National Institute of Mental Health, NIMH). Some genetic conditions, such as Down syndrome and fragile X syndrome, are linked to autism |
| Parents' age | Older parents are linked to a higher likelihood (WHO, NIMH) |
| Pregnancy | Diabetes in the mother, and exposure to some air pollutants or heavy metals before birth (WHO) |
| Certain medicines in pregnancy | Valproate and carbamazepine, used for epilepsy and other conditions (WHO). Don't stop any prescribed medicine on your own; talk to your doctor |
| Birth | Being born premature, very low birth weight, or severe birth complications (WHO, NIMH) |

### What does not cause autism

- **Vaccines.** WHO says a "robust, extensive evidence base" shows childhood vaccines do not cause autism. The study that claimed a link to the MMR vaccine was found to be fraudulent and was withdrawn. Vaccines with thiomersal or aluminium have not been shown to raise the risk either. Keep to your child's immunisation schedule.
- **Paracetamol in pregnancy.** In September 2025 WHO said there is no conclusive evidence of a link and no consistent association.

## How and where to get an autism assessment in India

Don't wait for a child to "outgrow" a delay. WHO says timely, evidence-based psychosocial support can improve communication and social skills.

1. **Talk to a paediatrician.** The IAP recommends that all children be screened with a standard autism screening tool at **18 and 24 months**. Tools it names include the M-CHAT and the Trivandrum Autism Behavior Checklist.
2. **Use RBSK and the DEIC.** Under the Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK), health teams screen children from birth to 18 years for the "4 Ds", one of which is developmental delays including disabilities. Children can be referred to the DEIC at the district hospital. DEICs focus mainly on children up to 6 years and have a team that can include a paediatrician, psychologist, and speech, occupational and physiotherapists, with family support for autism; older children are treated through other government health facilities, with the DEIC as a referral link.
3. **Get a full evaluation.** Diagnosis is usually made by a team: a developmental paediatrician, child psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, with speech and occupational therapists. A hearing test is often part of it.
4. **Adults can be assessed too.** NIMH notes autism can be diagnosed at any age. Ask the psychiatry department of a government medical college or a large hospital.

### Disability certificate and UDID card

Autism is one of the 21 disabilities listed in the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016. A person assessed at **40% or more** has a "benchmark disability". This brings rights such as free education from 6 to 18 years in a neighbourhood or special school (Section 31) and a share of the reserved posts in government jobs (Section 34).

Apply on the government's [UDID portal](https://www.swavlambancard.gov.in/). The district Chief Medical Officer's office or medical board assesses the person and issues the disability certificate and Unique Disability ID card. For a global overview, see the [WHO fact sheet on autism](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/autism-spectrum-disorders).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can autism be cured?

No. The NHS says there is no cure, and autism is not a disease. Be wary of anyone selling a "cure", special diets or supplements. Early, evidence-based support can help communication, learning and independence.

### At what age can autism be diagnosed?

NIMH says autism can usually be reliably diagnosed by about age 2, and signs are often seen earlier. It can be diagnosed at any age, and many people are only identified in school or adulthood.

### Do vaccines cause autism?

No. WHO says extensive research shows childhood vaccines, including MMR, do not cause autism.

### Is a child who talks late always autistic?

No. Late talking has many causes, including hearing problems and speech delay. A child who talks late and also shows little social interest, doesn't respond to their name or loses skills should be checked by a paediatrician.
