**Quick answer:** In India, high blood pressure (hypertension) means a reading of 140/90 mmHg or more, confirmed on two different days. That is the cut-off used by the World Health Organization (WHO) and India's national programme. US guidelines label readings from 130/80 as "stage 1" hypertension, so the same number can be described differently depending on which chart you read.

Most people with high BP feel completely normal, which is why it is called a silent condition. The only way to know is to measure it. If you are 30 or older, you can get it checked free at a government Ayushman Arogya Mandir or primary health centre.

## What the two numbers mean

The top number (systolic) is the pressure in your arteries when the heart beats; the bottom number (diastolic) is the pressure between beats.

According to the [WHO fact sheet on hypertension](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hypertension), hypertension is diagnosed when readings on two different days show a systolic of 140 or more and/or a diastolic of 90 or more. One high reading is not a diagnosis: BP rises with stress, a rushed walk, tea or a full bladder.

## Why charts disagree: India vs US vs Europe

Many Indian readers find American charts online and worry when 132/84 is called "hypertension". Here is how the main guidelines compare:

| Reading (mmHg) | WHO and India's NP-NCD | US (AHA/ACC 2025) | Europe (ESC 2024) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Below 120/80 | Normal | Normal | Non-elevated (below 120/70) |
| 120–129 / below 80 | Normal | Elevated | Elevated |
| 130–139 / 80–89 | Below the hypertension cut-off | Stage 1 hypertension | Elevated |
| 140/90 or more | Hypertension | Stage 2 hypertension | Hypertension |
| Above 180/120 | Hypertension (seek care urgently if symptoms) | Severe hypertension | Hypertension |

ESC 2024 counts 120–139 systolic or 70–89 diastolic as "elevated".

All three agree that 140/90 and above needs a doctor. The US and European guidelines flag the 120–139 range earlier because heart attack and stroke risk starts rising there. Treat readings in that band as an early warning to change habits; your doctor decides whether treatment is needed based on your overall risk.

## How common is high BP in India?

- **About 1 in 3 adults.** The ICMR-INDIAB study, published in *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* in 2023, found hypertension in 35.5% of 1.13 lakh people surveyed across 31 states and Union Territories.
- **24% of men and 21% of women** aged 15 and above had BP of 140/90 or more in the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5).
- **Only 37% were diagnosed and about 30% treated**, according to WHO's first global hypertension report (2023), which estimated 18.83 crore Indians aged 30–79 have the condition.
- **Only 12% have their BP under control**, according to WHO's India country page, which puts the number living with hypertension at about 22 crore.

The age pattern in NFHS-5 is worth knowing: about 1 in 5 men in their 30s already had high BP, and almost half of senior citizens did. Waiting until 50 for your first check is too late for many people.

## Symptoms: usually none, sometimes an emergency

Most people with high BP have no symptoms at all. Headaches and dizziness are not reliable signs, and feeling fine does not mean your BP is normal. The NHS lists South Asian background as a risk factor in itself, along with age, family history, a salty diet, excess weight, smoking, alcohol and long-term stress.

Over years, uncontrolled BP can lead to heart attack, heart failure, stroke and kidney failure, according to WHO.

### When it is an emergency (hypertensive crisis)

A reading above 180/120 is dangerous. The American Heart Association advises waiting a minute and measuring again. If it is still that high, check for symptoms.

**Go to the nearest emergency department or call 112 or 108 immediately** if a reading above 180/120 comes with any of these:

- Chest pain or pressure
- Breathlessness
- Back pain
- Numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg
- Sudden change in vision
- Difficulty speaking or confusion
- Severe headache with nausea or vomiting

Don't wait to see if it settles, and don't take extra doses of any medicine on your own. If the reading is that high but you have no symptoms, contact your doctor the same day.

## How to check your BP correctly at home

A digital upper-arm monitor is the most practical option for home use. Small mistakes in how you sit or wear the cuff can push the reading up, so technique matters. The US CDC recommends:

1. No tea, coffee, smoking, food, exercise or alcohol for 30 minutes before.
2. Use the toilet first. A full bladder raises the reading.
3. Sit quietly in a chair with your back supported for 5 minutes.
4. Keep both feet flat on the floor, legs uncrossed.
5. Rest your arm on a table so the cuff is at chest level.
6. Wrap the cuff snugly on your bare upper arm, not over a sleeve.
7. Don't talk or look at your phone during the reading.
8. Take two readings 1–2 minutes apart, at the same time each day, and note them down.

One thing to know: home readings run slightly lower than clinic readings. The NHS uses 135/85 as the high-BP mark for home readings, against 140/90 in a clinic. Take your log, and the machine itself, to your doctor once so they can check it against theirs.

## Salt: the Indian problem in numbers

WHO recommends less than 5 grams of salt a day, just under one teaspoon, and that includes salt already in food. Indians eat far more. A study by ICMR's National Centre for Disease Informatics and Research, based on urine tests from the National NCD Monitoring Survey (2017–18), found adults eat about 8 grams a day on average: 8.9 g for men and 7.1 g for women.

Much of it is hidden in everyday foods. Pickles, papad, namkeen, chutney powders, instant noodles, sauces, bakery items and the extra salt sprinkled on fruit, curd or salad add up quickly.

Ways to cut back:

- Add salt at the end of cooking and taste first. Lemon, garlic, ginger, herbs and spices add flavour.
- Keep pickles and papad as occasional extras, not daily sides.
- Read labels on packaged food: 5 g of salt equals 2,000 mg of sodium.

Other changes with good evidence, according to WHO and the NHS: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, more vegetables and fruit, losing extra weight, especially around the waist (see [how to reduce belly fat](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/belly-fat)), avoiding tobacco, limiting alcohol, and [managing long-term stress](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/stress-management).

## Free BP screening and medicines in India

Under the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD), people aged 30 and above are screened for high BP, diabetes and common cancers at government facilities.

- **Where:** Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (the renamed Health and Wellness Centres), primary health centres and community health centres. The Health Ministry reported 1,80,906 Ayushman Arogya Mandirs functioning as of 31 October 2025, and about 38.79 crore hypertension screenings done till then.
- **Treatment:** In a Rajya Sabha reply in August 2025, the Health Ministry said patients diagnosed under NP-NCD get free medicines and regular follow-up at these facilities.
- **The India Hypertension Control Initiative (IHCI)**, launched in 2017 by the Health Ministry, ICMR, state governments and WHO India, built this system of standard treatment and monthly refills. Separately, in May 2023 the Health Ministry launched the "75/25" initiative to put 7.5 crore people with high BP or diabetes on standard care by 2025.


## When to see a doctor

- Your readings are 140/90 or more on two different days.
- Home readings are regularly 135/85 or more.
- You are under 40 with high readings. Doctors may look for an underlying cause such as kidney or hormone problems.
- You have diabetes or kidney disease. Your target may be different (see our guide on [diabetes diagnosis and treatment](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/diabetes-diagnosis-and-treatment)).
- You are pregnant and have any high reading.
- You are on BP medicine and readings stay high.

Never stop or change BP medicine on your own because a reading looks normal. Normal readings often mean the medicine is working.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is 130/80 high blood pressure?

Under WHO and Indian practice, 130/80 is not hypertension, but it is above the ideal of under 120/80. US guidelines call it stage 1 hypertension, so treat it as a sign to cut salt, move more and recheck regularly.

### Can high blood pressure be cured?

When there is an underlying cause, such as a kidney or hormone problem, treating it can bring BP down. Most people have "primary" hypertension, which is controlled rather than cured, through lifestyle changes and, if your doctor advises, regular medicine.

### How often should I check my blood pressure?

If you are 30 or older, get it checked at least once a year (free under NP-NCD at an Ayushman Arogya Mandir), and more often if you are overweight, have diabetes or have a family history. If you have high BP, your doctor will tell you how often to measure at home.

### Is rock salt or pink salt better for blood pressure?

No. Rock salt, pink salt and sea salt are mostly sodium chloride, like table salt. What matters is the total amount, which WHO says should be under 5 grams a day.
