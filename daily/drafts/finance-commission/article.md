**Quick answer:** The Finance Commission is a body the President sets up every five years under Article 280 of the Constitution to recommend how the Centre's tax money is split with the states. The 16th Finance Commission, chaired by economist Arvind Panagariya, covers 2026-27 to 2030-31. It kept the states' share at **41% of the divisible pool** of central taxes, and the Union government accepted that.

The split happens in two steps. First, the "vertical" share decides how much goes to all states together (41%). Then a "horizontal" formula decides each state's slice, based on income, population, area, forest cover, population control and, for the first time, each state's contribution to national GDP. Uttar Pradesh gets the largest slice, 17.62 out of every 100 rupees devolved.

## Why India needs a Finance Commission

States run most everyday services (police, hospitals, schools, roads), but the Centre collects the biggest taxes, such as income tax, corporation tax and the central half of GST. States also differ hugely in what they can raise (see our explainer on [per capita income in India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/per-capita-income-india)). So the Constitution creates a neutral body to recommend the split, instead of leaving it to the Centre each year.

Article 280 sets up the Commission (a chairperson and four members), Article 270 says central taxes are shared with states, and Article 275 allows grants-in-aid to states that need extra help.

## Step 1: how much goes to states together

The pot that gets shared is called the **divisible pool**. It is the Centre's gross tax revenue minus three things: the cost of collecting taxes, taxes from Union Territories, and all cesses and surcharges.

The 16th Finance Commission recommended that states get **41%** of this pool, the same as the 15th Commission. Many states had asked for 50%, according to reports of their memoranda. According to the government's explanatory memorandum laid in Parliament on 1 February 2026, "The Government has accepted the above recommendations of the Commission" on the 41% share.

For 2026-27, the Union Budget estimates the states' share of central taxes at **₹15,26,255 crore**, according to PRS Legislative Research's Union Budget analysis. Gross tax revenue is budgeted at **₹44.04 lakh crore**. That means states get roughly **35% of everything the Centre collects** in taxes, not 41%. (This is our calculation from the two budget figures.)

### Why 41% becomes about 35%: cesses and surcharges

A cess is a tax for a specific purpose (like the health and education cess on income tax); a surcharge is an extra tax on a tax (like the one on very high incomes). Under Articles 270 and 271, the Centre keeps 100% of both.

When cesses and surcharges grow, the divisible pool shrinks even if the 41% stays the same. According to the 16th Finance Commission's report, as cited in *The India Forum*, the divisible pool fell from 89.1% of gross tax revenue in 2014-15 to between 74% and 80% in later years. The Commission said there was "no room" to cut the states' share and suggested a future "grand bargain": the Centre would fold much of its cess and surcharge revenue into regular, shareable taxes, and in return states would accept a smaller percentage of the larger divisible pool, so neither side loses revenue.

## Step 2: how each state's share is worked out

Once the 41% is fixed, each state's slice is decided by a weighted formula. Here is how the 16th Commission changed the weights compared with the 15th, according to PRS Legislative Research's summary of the report.

| Criterion | What it measures | 15th FC (2021-26) | 16th FC (2026-31) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income distance | How far a state's per capita GSDP is below the average of the top three states by per capita GSDP; poorer states get more | 45% | 42.5% |
| Population | State's share of India's population, Census 2011 | 15% | 17.5% |
| Demographic performance | Rewards states that controlled population growth (16th FC: growth 1971-2011; 15th FC used fertility rate) | 12.5% | 10% |
| Area | Geographical area, with a minimum floor for small states | 15% | 10% |
| Forest | Forest cover, now including open forest, plus a small reward for growth in forest area | 10% | 10% |
| Tax and fiscal effort | How well a state raises its own taxes | 2.5% | Dropped |
| Contribution to GDP | Square root of a state's GSDP as a share of the total; new criterion | Not used | 10% |

Income distance uses average per capita GSDP for 2018-19 to 2023-24, leaving out the Covid year 2020-21. The minimum area share assumed for tiny states was cut from 2% to 1.5%. The square root in the GDP criterion stops the largest economies from taking a runaway share; the Commission says it captures "the effect of various forms of efficiencies".

### Who gained and who lost

Ratings agency ICRA calculated that 14 of the 28 states get a larger share than before, with Karnataka gaining the most. Shares for some large states, according to PRS:

## Grants: the other half of the award

Tax sharing is untied money that states can spend as they choose. The Commission also recommends grants. For 2026-31, according to PRS, the 16th Commission recommended **₹9.47 lakh crore** in grants:

- **₹7.91 lakh crore for panchayats and municipalities**, about double the ₹3.9 lakh crore actually released to local bodies over 2021-26, according to ICRA.
- **₹1.56 lakh crore** as the Centre's share for state disaster response and mitigation funds.

The bigger change is what is missing. The 15th Commission gave about ₹2.9 lakh crore in **revenue deficit grants** to 17 states, according to ICRA. The 16th Commission recommended none. Its report argues that such grants "create an adverse incentive" and encourage states to be "profligate" instead of fixing the causes of their deficits. The government's memorandum only "takes note" of this part, rather than formally accepting it.

## What happened to GST compensation?

When GST replaced VAT and other state taxes in July 2017, the Centre promised to make up any shortfall if a state's GST revenue grew by less than 14% a year, for five years. That guarantee ended on 30 June 2022. The compensation cess was then kept on, mainly to repay money borrowed to pay states during the Covid years. According to PRS, it was largely removed from September 2025 and ended on tobacco and pan masala in February 2026, so it is no longer a source of money for states.

GST itself is split differently: the state half (SGST) stays with the state, while the central half (CGST) goes into the divisible pool.

## "We pay more than we get back": the debate

This is the most contested part of the system, and both sides have a case.

### What richer states argue

- **They get back far less than they contribute.** The Karnataka government told the 16th Commission in August 2024 that it gets back about 15 paise for every rupee it contributes in Union taxes, according to the *Deccan Chronicle*, and proposed that each state keep about 60% of what it contributes.
- **Population weight can penalise states that slowed population growth.** States with lower birth rates argue that a large weight on population rewards faster-growing states, even when a demographic criterion is added to offset this.
- **Cesses and surcharges shrink the pool.** Because these stay with the Centre, states lose out as cesses and surcharges take a bigger share of central taxes. Most states, not only richer ones, make this point. Karnataka's government asked the Commission in August 2024 to cap cesses and surcharges at 5% of gross tax revenue, according to PTI.

### What the other side argues

- **Where tax is paid is not always where income is earned.** A company headquartered in Mumbai or Bengaluru typically pays its taxes there, even if its customers and factories are spread across India. Economist Pinaki Chakraborty, in a 2024 study in *The India Forum*, argued that sharing by place of collection would widen gaps between states.
- **Equalisation is the point.** The income distance criterion exists so that poorer states, which cannot raise enough on their own, can still offer basic schooling and healthcare.
- **The formula has already moved.** The same 2024 study showed Bihar's and Uttar Pradesh's shares falling across recent Commissions. Separately, the 16th Commission's new GDP criterion raised the shares of Karnataka, Maharashtra and others. A review in *The India Forum* also found that the combined weight on population and income distance fell from between 72.5% and 77.5% under the 11th to 14th Commissions to 60% under the 15th, and the 16th kept it at 60%.

## How this connects to the Union Budget

The Commission recommends the formula for five years (the government accepted the 16th Commission's), but the actual rupees depend on how much tax the Centre collects, which is why the states' share appears each February in the Budget (see our [Union Budget explainer](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/union-budget-2024)). The government's [explanatory memorandum on the 16th Finance Commission report](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/doc/16fc.pdf) lists what it accepted.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Finance Commission of India?

It is a constitutional body under Article 280, set up by the President every five years, that recommends how central taxes are shared between the Centre and states and among the states. It also recommends grants to states and local bodies.

### What share of central taxes do states get?

States get 41% of the divisible pool for 2026-31, as recommended by the 16th Finance Commission and accepted by the government. Because cesses and surcharges are not shared, this works out to about 35% of gross tax revenue in 2026-27.

### Who chairs the 16th Finance Commission?

Economist Arvind Panagariya chairs it. The Commission submitted its report to the President on 17 November 2025, and the report was tabled in Parliament on 1 February 2026.

### Are the Finance Commission's recommendations binding?

No, they are advisory. In practice the government has usually accepted the tax-sharing formula, as it did for the 16th Commission, but it can accept other parts only "in principle" or examine them later.
