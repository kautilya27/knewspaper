**Quick answer:** Fundamental Rights (Part III of the Constitution, Articles 12 to 35) are guarantees you can enforce in court. If the State violates one, you can go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32 or to a High Court under Article 226. Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36 to 51) are goals the State must keep in mind while making laws, but no court can order the government to carry them out.

Article 37 says this in one sentence: the Directive Principles "shall not be enforceable by any court", yet they are "fundamental in the governance of the country" and the State has a duty to apply them in making laws. One part limits what the State may do to you; the other says what it should work towards.

## The core difference in one table

| | Fundamental Rights | Directive Principles |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Part III, Articles 12–35 | Part IV, Articles 36–51 |
| Can you go to court? | Yes (justiciable) | No (non-justiciable, Article 37) |
| Nature | Mostly limits on the State ("the State shall not…") | Instructions to the State ("the State shall endeavour…") |
| Aim | Liberty and equality | Social and economic welfare |
| Laws that conflict | Can be struck down under Article 13 | A law is not void just because it ignores a Directive Principle |

## The six groups of Fundamental Rights

1. **Right to equality (Articles 14–18):** equality before law, no discrimination, equal chance in public jobs, end of untouchability.
2. **Right to freedom (Articles 19–22):** the Article 19 freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence and profession; life and personal liberty (Article 21); education for ages 6–14 (Article 21A); safeguards on arrest.
3. **Right against exploitation (Articles 23–24):** no trafficking, forced labour or child labour in hazardous work.
4. **Right to freedom of religion (Articles 25–28).**
5. **Cultural and educational rights (Articles 29–30):** minorities' culture and their right to run educational institutions.
6. **Right to constitutional remedies (Article 32):** approaching the Supreme Court is itself a Fundamental Right.

There were seven groups in 1950. The **44th Amendment (1978)** removed the right to property by deleting Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31, and placed a narrower version in Article 300A. Property is now a constitutional right, not a fundamental one, so you can't go directly to the Supreme Court under Article 32 over it. The same amendment made Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable during an emergency.

Courts have also read the right to information into the freedom of speech in Article 19(1)(a), the idea behind the RTI Act, which you can use yourself ([here is how to file an RTI online](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-file-rti-online)).

## Key Directive Principles, article by article

- **Article 39:** livelihood for all, sharing the "material resources of the community" for the common good (39(b)), no concentration of wealth (39(c)), equal pay for equal work (39(d)).
- **Article 39A:** free legal aid.
- **Article 40:** village panchayats as units of self-government.
- **Article 41:** right to work, education and public assistance, "within the limits of its economic capacity".
- **Article 44:** a uniform civil code throughout India.
- **Article 45:** early childhood care and education below age six.
- **Article 46:** educational and economic interests of SCs, STs and weaker sections.
- **Article 47:** nutrition, public health, and moving towards prohibition of intoxicants.
- **Article 48A:** protect the environment, forests and wildlife.

Article 44 is the most debated, but like the rest it is a directive: no court can order Parliament to enact a uniform civil code.

## Non-enforceable, but not toothless: DPSPs that became laws

What use is a principle you can't enforce? Look at the statute book. Once a Directive Principle is turned into a law or amendment, it becomes enforceable through that law.

| Directive Principle | What it asks for | What it led to |
|---|---|---|
| Article 40 | Village panchayats | 73rd Amendment Act, 1992: Part IX, elected panchayats with fixed terms and reserved seats. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons cites Article 40. |
| Article 45 (original) | Free and compulsory education to age 14 | 86th Amendment, 2002 made it a Fundamental Right (Article 21A); the RTE Act, 2009 followed, in force from 1 April 2010 |
| Article 41 | Right to work | National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (renamed MGNREGA in 2009; 100 days of wage work for rural households). Replaced from 1 July 2026 by the VB-G RAM G Act, 2025 (125 days), according to PIB |
| Article 47 | Nutrition and public health | National Food Security Act, 2013. The Bill's Statement of Objects and Reasons quotes Article 47 |
| Article 39(d) | Equal pay for equal work | Equal Remuneration Act, 1976; the same rule against gender pay discrimination is now in the Code on Wages, 2019 |
| Article 39A | Free legal aid | Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 |

The new jobs law is debated: some states and critics worry that its 60:40 Centre-state funding split raises states' costs, while the Centre says it widens the guarantee.

## When the two clashed: four cases in plain language

Most of the conflict was over land: governments wanted to redistribute it under Article 39(b) and (c); owners said this violated their rights to property and equality.

### State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): rights come first

Madras's "Communal G.O." fixed college seats by religion and caste. A seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court struck it down on 9 April 1951 for violating Article 29(2), which bars denial of admission on grounds of religion, race, caste or language. In the judgment's words, the Directive Principles "have to conform to and run as subsidiary to the Chapter of Fundamental Rights". Parliament responded with the First Amendment (1951), adding Article 15(4) to allow special provisions for backward classes, SCs and STs.

### Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967): rights cannot be amended away

According to the judgment text on the Supreme Court's website, on 27 February 1967 an 11-judge bench ruled 6–5 that Parliament could not use its amending power to take away or abridge Fundamental Rights. Using "prospective overruling", it left earlier amendments intact. Parliament replied in 1971 with the 24th Amendment, reasserting its power to amend any part, and the 25th, which inserted **Article 31C**: laws giving effect to Article 39(b) or (c) could not be challenged for violating Articles 14, 19 or 31.

### Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): the basic structure

On 24 April 1973 a 13-judge bench, by 7–6 (as LiveLaw's account of the case records), overruled Golaknath: Parliament *can* amend Fundamental Rights, but not the Constitution's "basic structure". On Article 31C, the court upheld its first part but struck down the clause that stopped courts from asking whether a law actually gave effect to those Directive Principles.

### Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): balance is the basic structure

The 42nd Amendment (1976) widened Article 31C to protect laws implementing *any* Directive Principle, not just 39(b) and (c), from challenge under Articles 14, 19 or 31. In 1980 (order on 9 May, reasons on 31 July) a five-judge bench, by 4–1 on Article 31C, struck that expansion down (with Section 55, which curbed review of amendments). The court said the Constitution rests on "the balance between Parts III and IV", and that "to give absolute primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution". The balance between the two Parts is itself part of the basic structure.

A footnote that many exam notes miss: in *Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra* (5 November 2024), a nine-judge bench held that the original Article 31C, as upheld in Kesavananda, still stands. The majority also held that not all private property is a "material resource of the community" under Article 39(b), according to the Supreme Court Observer.

## Where Fundamental Duties fit

Fundamental Duties are a third piece, in Part IVA (Article 51A). The 42nd Amendment (1976) added ten, such as respecting the Constitution, Flag and Anthem, protecting the environment and developing a scientific temper. The 86th Amendment (2002) added an eleventh: parents and guardians must give children aged 6 to 14 the opportunity to be educated. Like the Directive Principles, they are not directly enforceable. They are worth rereading around [Republic Day, when the Constitution came into force](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-is-republic-day-celebrated).

The full text of each article is on the website of the [Legislative Department, Ministry of Law and Justice](https://legislative.gov.in/constitution-of-india).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I go to court if the government ignores a Directive Principle?

No, Article 37 makes them non-enforceable. But if a Directive Principle has been made into a law, such as the RTE Act or the food security law, you can enforce the rights that law gives you.

### Which is more important, Fundamental Rights or Directive Principles?

Neither has absolute priority. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Supreme Court held that the two must be read in harmony, and that this balance is part of the Constitution's basic structure.

### Is the right to property a Fundamental Right in India?

No. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed it from Part III. It now sits in Article 300A, which says no one shall be deprived of property except by authority of law.

### Are Fundamental Duties enforceable?

Not directly. Article 51A lists 11 duties, but there is no general penalty for not following them, though some separate laws, such as the one on insulting the National Flag, carry penalties.
