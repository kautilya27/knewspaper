**Quick answer:** The Eisenhower Principle says you should sort tasks by two separate questions: *Is it urgent?* (does it need attention now?) and *Is it important?* (does it move your real goals forward?). Putting those two questions together gives a four-box grid, the **Eisenhower matrix**: do urgent and important tasks now, schedule important ones that aren't urgent, delegate or shrink urgent ones that aren't important, and drop the rest.

The idea matters because urgency is loud and importance is quiet. Research on the "mere urgency effect" (more on it below) shows that people often pick a task just because it has a deadline, even when another task would pay off more. The matrix is a way to catch yourself doing that.

## The four boxes of the Eisenhower matrix

| | **Urgent** | **Not urgent** |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | **1. Do now.** Crises, real deadlines, problems that get worse if left | **2. Schedule.** Planning, learning, health, relationships, building skills |
| **Not important** | **3. Delegate, batch or shorten.** Most interruptions, many calls and messages, other people's small requests | **4. Drop.** Mindless scrolling, busywork, tasks no one will miss |

Two definitions:

- **Urgent** means time-sensitive: someone or something is asking for it now, and delay has a visible cost soon.
- **Important** means it connects to what you are trying to achieve: marks, income, health, family, a career move. Importance is about results, not about who is asking.

Box 2 is the one that pays off most over time, and the one most people neglect. Nothing forces you to do it this week, so it slides until it becomes a Box 1 emergency.

## Where the idea really comes from

The name is borrowed from Dwight D. Eisenhower, the US Army general who became US President. According to Quote Investigator, which traced the line to its source, Eisenhower spoke at the Second Assembly of the World Council of Churches on the campus of Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois, on 19 August 1954, and said:

> "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent."

Two details often get lost. First, Eisenhower did not claim the saying as his own: he attributed it to an unnamed "former college president". Second, there is no record that he drew a four-box matrix. The grid became famous through Stephen Covey's 1989 book *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, where Habit 3, "Put First Things First", sorts time into four quadrants by urgency and importance in what he called the Time Management Matrix. Covey's "Quadrant II" (important, not urgent) is the same as Box 2 above. The name "Eisenhower matrix" was later attached to this grid.

## What research says: the mere urgency effect

In 2018, Meng Zhu (Johns Hopkins Carey Business School), Yang Yang and Christopher K. Hsee published "The Mere Urgency Effect" in the *Journal of Consumer Research* (volume 45, issue 3). Across five experiments, as [Johns Hopkins University reported](https://hub.jhu.edu/2018/05/31/meeting-deadlines-time-management-behaviors), people often chose a less important task with a short time window over a more important one with a better payoff.

Key points from the study:

- The pull came from a **perceived expiration**, not from the urgent task being easier or giving a faster reward. The researchers ruled those explanations out.
- The effect was **stronger in people who see themselves as busy**.
- **Reminding people of the payoff** of each task at the moment of choosing reduced the effect.

That last finding is the practical one: the matrix makes you ask "what does this achieve?" before you act, which is the kind of payoff reminder the study found helpful.

## Worked examples for Indian readers

### A Class 12 student, six weeks before board exams

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | Practical file due to the school on Monday; revising the chapter for tomorrow's pre-board | Solving full sample papers under time; fixing weak topics in Physics; sleeping on time |
| **Not important** | Replying to every message in the class group; a cousin's request to help with a form today | Watching exam "strategy" reels; reorganising notes with new colours |

The pre-board and the practical file feel like the whole job, but marks are mostly decided in Box 2: timed practice and weak topics. Block a fixed daily slot for Box 2 work before opening the phone. Our guide on [how to stop procrastinating](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-stop-procrastinating) has more on starting tasks you keep avoiding.

### A software engineer in an IT company

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | A production bug affecting users; a client demo tomorrow | Writing tests and documentation; learning a skill for your next role; preparing for the appraisal cycle |
| **Not important** | Most meeting invites where you are optional; status pings that an update in the team tracker would answer | Endless tab-switching between chat and email; tidying dashboards no one reads |

Box 3 is usually the biggest drain here. You often can't delegate as a junior engineer, so "delegate" becomes *batch* (check chat at fixed times), *shorten* (ask for the agenda before joining) or *redirect* (point people to the tracker).

### A small shop owner (a kirana or mobile-recharge shop)

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | A supplier waiting for payment today; the UPI scanner not working during peak hours | Keeping a simple record of daily sales and dues; checking which items sell slowly; renewing licences or registrations before they lapse |
| **Not important** | Long calls from sales agents; a non-regular customer asking for a discount discussion right now | Rearranging shelves that already work |

For a shop owner, Box 2 is often bookkeeping. Ten minutes of daily record-keeping can prevent a cash crunch, which is a Box 1 crisis.

## How to use the matrix

1. **List everything** on your plate for the next week.
2. **Ask "important?" first**, then "urgent?". Starting with importance stops deadlines from deciding for you.
3. **Put Box 2 tasks on your calendar** with a specific time, not just on a list.
4. **Decide how to handle Box 3**: hand off, batch, shorten or say no politely.
5. **Delete Box 4** without guilt.
6. **Review once a week.** If Box 1 is always full, ask which Box 2 task would have prevented those fires.

Small, repeated Box 2 actions add up, which is the point of [small habits that outperform big goals](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/small-habits-big-changes-why-tiny-daily-actions-outperform-ambitious-goals).

## Where the Eisenhower matrix doesn't work well

The grid is a thinking aid, not a law. Be honest about its limits:

- **"Important" is subjective.** The matrix doesn't tell you what matters. If your goals are unclear, you'll sort tasks by habit. Settle priorities first.
- **Delegation assumes you have someone to delegate to.** Students, freelancers and junior employees often don't. Use batching and saying no instead.
- **It ignores effort and order.** Two Box 2 tasks may differ hugely in size, and some tasks must happen before others. The matrix doesn't sequence work.
- **Real life is a sliding scale.** Urgency and importance aren't yes-or-no, so borderline items fit badly.
- **Some urgent things are important to other people.** A parent's hospital visit or a manager's escalation may not fit your goals but still deserves time. Don't use the matrix to justify ignoring obligations.
- **It isn't a fix for burnout.** If Box 1 never empties because the workload itself is too large, sorting won't help; the conversation is with your manager or family about load. See our piece on [stress management](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/stress-management).

## Frequently asked questions

### Did Eisenhower invent the Eisenhower matrix?

No. In a 1954 speech at Northwestern University he quoted an unnamed former college president about urgent and important problems. Stephen Covey turned the idea into a four-quadrant grid in his 1989 book *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*.

### What is the difference between urgent and important?

Urgent tasks demand attention now because of a deadline or someone's request. Important tasks contribute to your long-term goals, whether or not anyone is pushing you to do them.

### Which quadrant should I spend most time in?

Aim to spend as much time as you can in important but not urgent work (Box 2, Covey's Quadrant II), such as planning, practice and learning. Doing it early keeps many tasks from becoming emergencies.

### What is the mere urgency effect?

It is a finding by Zhu, Yang and Hsee, published in the *Journal of Consumer Research* in 2018: people often choose urgent but less valuable tasks over more valuable ones simply because the urgent task seems about to expire. Reminding yourself of each task's payoff reduces it.
