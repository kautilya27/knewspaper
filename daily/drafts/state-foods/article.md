**Quick answer:** Every Indian state and union territory has a dish it is best known for: litti chokha in Bihar, dal baati churma in Rajasthan, sarson da saag with makki di roti in Punjab, fish curry and rice in Goa, sadya in Kerala, jadoh in Meghalaya, rogan josh and the wazwan feast in Kashmir. The tables below cover all 28 states and 8 union territories, with a signature dish, the everyday staple and a well-known sweet for each.

The last column lists food items with a **Geographical Indication (GI) tag**, India's legal protection for products tied to a place, like Bikaneri bhujia or Kashmir saffron. These come from the state-wise list of registered food and agri GIs kept by APEDA, the Commerce Ministry's export body, plus a few registered since. Where we couldn't confirm a GI food, the cell says "none found", not a guess.

One caveat before the tables: food in India changes every hundred kilometres. Coastal Karnataka eats very differently from north Karnataka, and Jammu's kitchen is not Kashmir's. Treat each row as a starting point, not the whole story.

## How to read the tables

- **Signature dish**: what the state is best known for outside its borders.
- **Staple**: what most homes actually eat every day.
- **Sweet**: a dessert or mithai closely tied to the place. In several northeastern states sweets play only a small part in a meal, and the table says so rather than inventing one.
- **GI-tagged food (examples)**: up to five registered names. Many states have more. Basmati rice holds a single shared GI that covers parts of Punjab, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Delhi, Jammu & Kashmir, Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand.

## What a GI tag does and doesn't mean

A GI tag protects a *name*, not a recipe. Only producers from the registered area who follow its specification can sell "Bikaneri Bhujia" or "Kandhamal Haladi" under that name. It doesn't mean the food is healthier, and it doesn't stop anyone making bhujia elsewhere under another name.

The tag also misses a lot. Litti chokha, dal baati, misal pav and sadya have no GI because they are dishes cooked everywhere, not products tied to one place. If you want to check a name yourself, APEDA's [list of registered food and agri GIs](https://apeda.gov.in/node/79) is organised by state. Its catalogue runs to May 2024, so newer tags (such as Jharkhand's and the Andaman ones above) appear first in Registry records and news. Some tea and coffee GIs, such as Darjeeling Tea (registered to the Tea Board), don't appear on APEDA's list, though it does include spices like Kandhamal Haladi and Guntur Sannam chilli.

Many of these GI items are spices and chillies, from Guntur Sannam to Kandhamal turmeric. If you're unsure what some of them are called in English, see our list of [spice names in English and Hindi](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/spices-names-in-english-and-hindi).

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Indian state has the most GI-tagged products?

Uttar Pradesh, with 81 GI registrations across all categories as of December 2025, followed by Tamil Nadu (76) and Maharashtra (55), according to the Press Information Bureau. Among food and agri GIs alone, Maharashtra's list on APEDA is the longest.

### Does rasgulla have a GI tag for Odisha or West Bengal?

Both. Banglar Rasogolla is registered for West Bengal and Odisha Rasagola for Odisha, as two separate GI names.

### What is the national dish of India?

India has no official national dish. Khichdi is often mentioned, but no government notification makes any dish "national".

### Which union territories have GI-tagged foods?

According to APEDA's state-wise list, Jammu & Kashmir has GIs including Kashmir saffron and Mushqbudji rice, Ladakh has its Raktsey Karpo apricot and seabuckthorn, and Delhi falls in the Basmati GI area. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands got three food GIs in December 2024. We found none for Chandigarh, Puducherry, Lakshadweep or Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu.
