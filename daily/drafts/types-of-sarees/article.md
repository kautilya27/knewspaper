**Quick answer:** India has dozens of regional handloom sarees, and around 50 sarees and saree silks are protected by name as Geographical Indications (GIs). The best known include Kancheepuram silk (Tamil Nadu), Banarasi (Uttar Pradesh), Paithani (Maharashtra), Patan Patola (Gujarat), Chanderi and Maheshwari (Madhya Pradesh), Baluchari (West Bengal), Sambalpuri and Bomkai (Odisha), Pochampally Ikat (Telangana), Mysore silk and Ilkal (Karnataka) and Muga silk (Assam).

You can usually tell them apart by three things: the fibre, how the border and pallu are woven, and the typical motifs. The table below lists them state by state, with the exact name and year from the official Geographical Indications Registry (IP India, Ministry of Commerce).

## What a GI tag on a saree actually means

A GI tag is registered under the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999. It protects a name, not a design. Once "Chanderi Sarees" is registered, only sarees woven in the defined Chanderi area by the traditional method can legally be sold under that name. A powerloom lookalike from elsewhere cannot be sold as Chanderi.

According to the Ministry of Textiles (PIB, August 2026), 106 handloom products, six product logos and 227 handicraft products are registered as GIs.

- **A GI is about place, the Silk Mark is about fibre.** A GI says where and how a saree was made. It does not certify that the silk is pure. For purity checks (Silk Mark label, burn test, real vs tested zari), see our guide on [how to identify a pure silk saree](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-identify-pure-silk-saree).
- **Popular names aren't always the registered names.** "Kanjeevaram" is registered as Kancheepuram Silk, "Ikkat from Pochampally" as Pochampalli Ikat, and "Banarasi" as Banaras Brocades and Sarees.

## Popular sarees that are not registered under that name

Some familiar names are not GI names, or are protected as a craft rather than a saree:

- **Kerala kasavu:** the off-white saree with a gold border is not registered as "kasavu". Kerala's registered sarees are Balaramapuram, Kasaragod and Kuthampully.
- **Bandhani:** registered as crafts, not sarees: Jamnagari Bandhani and Kutch Bandhani (Gujarat), and Jodhpur Bandhej Craft (Rajasthan). Gujarat's bridal Gharchola is registered as Gharchola Craft of Gujarat.
- **Chikankari, Phulkari and Kantha:** these are embroidery GIs (Lucknow Chikan Craft, Phulkari, Nakshi Kantha), so the tag covers the needlework, whatever base saree it is done on.
- **Nauvari:** a nine-yard Maharashtrian drape style, not a weave, so it has no GI.
- **Jamdani in general:** the registered name is Uppada Jamdani from Andhra Pradesh. Bengal jamdani is not listed under that name in the registry.

## How to tell lookalikes apart

Quick visual checks for sarees often confused in shops:

- **Kancheepuram vs Arani vs Dharmavaram:** Look where the border meets the body. In a true Kancheepuram the two are interlocked during weaving, usually in a clearly different colour. Arani lacks this interlocking. Dharmavaram carries heavy zari on the body too.
- **Patola vs Pochampally vs Sambalpuri:** All three are ikat. Patan Patola is double ikat with crisp, identical patterns on both faces. Pochampally shows a hazy edge on geometric shapes. Sambalpuri often has chequerboards and fish or conch figures.
- **Chanderi vs Maheshwari vs Kota Doria:** All are light silk-cotton. Kota Doria has square checks across the fabric; according to The Federal, handloom checks are uniform while powerloom copies make them bigger. Chanderi is sheer with small zari butis. Maheshwari usually has a zari border and zari stripes across the pallu.
- **Baluchari vs Banarasi:** Banarasi is zari-heavy and floral. A traditional Baluchari pallu shows story panels in silk thread, without zari.

A printed or powerloom copy can imitate the look, so the surest proof of origin is a GI logo where one exists (Pochampally, Banaras, Kota Doria, Muga and Mysore Silk have registered logos), the Handloom Mark, or an India Handloom Brand label. According to the Ministry of Textiles, 29,402 Handloom Mark registrations had been issued by June 2026.

## Why it matters when you buy

The Fourth All India Handloom Census (2019–20) counted 35.22 lakh handloom weavers and allied workers, more than 72% of them women, as quoted by PIB in August 2026. A powerloom copy sold under a GI name takes business from them. Fewer, longer-lasting handwoven sarees are also kinder to the planet than cheap trends; see [why fast fashion is bad for you and the environment](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/fast-fashion-bad-for-you-and-the-environment).

To check a name yourself, see the full list of registrations on the [IP India website](https://www.ipindia.gov.in/storage/uploads/docs-operator/aa9ef4c8-ae0e-4717-9181-52b6a9479b82.pdf), and the Ministry of Textiles' [National Handloom Day 2026 note](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/aug/doc202686945201.pdf) explains the Handloom Mark and India Handloom Brand.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which was the first saree to get a GI tag in India?

Pochampalli Ikat (Telangana) and Chanderi Sarees (Madhya Pradesh) are both in the registry's very first year of registrations, 2004–05. Kancheepuram Silk, Mysore Silk and Kota Doria followed in 2005–06.

### Which state has the most GI-tagged sarees?

Going by the IP India list, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal lead, with roughly ten and nine saree or saree-silk GIs respectively, followed by Odisha, then Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Jharkhand with about four each. Tamil Nadu's range runs from Kancheepuram silk to Woraiyur cotton, registered in 2025–26.

### Does a GI tag mean the saree is pure silk?

No. A GI confirms the place and method of making, not the purity of the fibre. For silk purity, look for the Silk Mark label and use the checks in our pure silk saree guide.

### Is the Banarasi saree GI-tagged?

Yes. It is registered as Banaras Brocades and Sarees (Uttar Pradesh) in 2009–10, and its logo was registered separately in 2012–13.
