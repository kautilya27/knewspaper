**Quick answer:** Most everyday Indian spices have simple English names: jeera is cumin, dhaniya is coriander seed, haldi is turmeric, saunf is fennel, ajwain is carom (bishop's weed), kalonji is nigella, javitri is mace and jaiphal is nutmeg. The table below gives 27 common spices in English and Hindi, plus Bengali, Gujarati, Marathi and Malayalam names and the botanical name that settles any argument.

The tricky part is the look-alikes. "Kala jeera" means one seed in a Kashmiri kitchen and another in a Bengali one, and some English labels on spice packets are simply wrong. We untangle the common mix-ups after the table.

## Commonly confused spice pairs

These are the mix-ups that cause the most trouble when you are following a recipe from another state or a foreign cookbook.

### Kalonji vs kala jeera vs shahi jeera

- **Kalonji** is nigella (*Nigella sativa*): small, matte, jet-black seeds with sharp corners. It belongs to the buttercup family and has nothing to do with onions, even though packets often call it "onion seed".
- **Shahi jeera** is the slim, curved, dark-brown seed of *Elwendia persica* (still widely sold as *Bunium persicum*). Its native range runs from Iran and Central Asia into the western Himalaya, which is why Bengali even calls it "Kashmiri jira". In North India it is also called **kala jeera**, and in English "black cumin" or "black caraway".
- In **Bengal**, "kalo jeere" (kala jeera) means **nigella**, the same seed as kalonji. So a Bengali recipe asking for kalo jeere in panch phoron wants nigella, not shahi jeera.

"Black cumin" is used for both seeds in different books, so the botanical name helps. A quick test: nigella is angular and slightly bitter. Shahi jeera looks like a thin, dark cumin seed and smells sweeter. Indian glossaries also often label shahi jeera "caraway", but true caraway (*Carum carvi*) is a related, different plant.

### Javitri vs jaiphal

Both come from the same fruit of the nutmeg tree, *Myristica fragrans*. **Jaiphal** (nutmeg) is the hard brown seed. **Javitri** (mace) is the red, lacy covering (aril) wrapped around that seed, which turns orange-tan as it dries. They taste related but are not the same. Mace is lighter and more delicate, which is why it shows up in biryani and korma masalas. ### Ajwain vs radhuni vs ajmod

- **Ajwain** (*Trachyspermum ammi*) is carom or bishop's weed, with a strong, thyme-like smell.
- **Radhuni** (*Psammogeton involucratum*, long sold as *Trachyspermum roxburghianum*) is a relative in the same carrot family, used mainly in Bengali cooking. In many West Bengal homes it takes the place of mustard seeds in panch phoron. It smells more like celery and parsley than ajwain does.
- **Ajmod** is the Hindi name given to radhuni, and also to celery seed (*Apium graveolens*). That is why glossaries that translate ajmod as "ajwain" mislead readers.

Malayalam names overlap too: "ayamodakam" is listed for ajwain in some references and for radhuni in others. When it matters, buy by the botanical name.

### Saunf vs sowa (fennel vs dill)

**Saunf** is fennel (*Foeniculum vulgare*): plump, pale green, ridged and sweet. **Sowa** or **soya** is dill (*Anethum graveolens*): flatter, lighter brown and oval, with a sharper taste. The Spices Board notes that Indian dill (*Anethum sowa*), native to northern India, has a bolder flavour than European dill. In Marathi, fresh dill leaves are **shepu**, a popular sabzi.

Aniseed (*Pimpinella anisum*, sometimes called vilayati saunf) is a third seed again. Some Gujarati and Marathi names (variyali, badishep) are used for both fennel and aniseed, so check the seed's colour and size.

### Kabab chini vs allspice

**Kabab chini** or **sheetal chini** is cubeb (*Piper cubeba*), a pepper berry with a little "tail" of stalk still attached. In Bengali and Hindi usage the name kabab chini is sometimes also applied to allspice (*Pimenta dioica*), a smooth, round berry without the tail. If the berry has a stalk, it is cubeb.

### Other pairs worth knowing

- **Dalchini vs cassia:** True cinnamon (*Cinnamomum verum*) has thin, papery layers that roll up together. Cassia (*Cinnamomum cassia*) is a thicker, harder single layer. Both are sold as dalchini, so look at the bark.
- **Tej patta vs bay leaf:** Indian bay leaf (*Cinnamomum tamala*) has three long veins running from base to tip and a cinnamon-like smell. The European bay leaf (*Laurus nobilis*) has one central vein. They are not swaps for each other in most recipes.
- **Chhoti vs badi elaichi:** Green cardamom is sweet and floral. Black cardamom is large, wrinkled and smoky, and belongs to a different genus.

## How to use this list when cooking or shopping

1. **Shop by the botanical name when a recipe matters.** It is the one label that does not change from state to state.
2. **Check the seed itself.** Shape, colour and smell tell you more than the packet's English label.
3. **Look for the basics on the packet.** Packaged food in India carries an FSSAI licence or registration number, and the label should show the name of the product and the packer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is kalonji called in English?

Kalonji is nigella, from the plant *Nigella sativa*. It is often sold as "onion seed" or "black seed", but it is not related to onion.

### Is kala jeera the same as kalonji?

It depends on where you are. In Bengal, kalo jeere usually means nigella (kalonji). In North India and Kashmir, kala jeera means shahi jeera, the seed of *Elwendia persica* (syn. *Bunium persicum*), which is a different spice.

### What is the difference between javitri and jaiphal?

Both come from the nutmeg tree. Jaiphal is the nutmeg seed, and javitri (mace) is the lacy red covering around that seed, dried separately.

### What is ajwain called in English?

Ajwain is called carom seed or bishop's weed in English, and its botanical name is *Trachyspermum ammi*. It is different from radhuni and celery seed, though the Hindi name ajmod is sometimes confused with it.
