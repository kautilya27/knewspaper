**Quick answer:** Stress management works best as a few small skills practised daily. The World Health Organization's free guide [Doing What Matters in Times of Stress](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240003927) teaches five (grounding, unhooking, acting on your values, being kind and making room), and WHO and the NHS back a handful of habits: a steady routine, regular sleep, physical activity, slow breathing, less news scrolling and talking to people you trust.

If stress keeps going after the pressure has passed, stops you from working, studying or sleeping, or you feel unable to cope, see a doctor. In India you can also call the government's free Tele-MANAS helpline on **14416** or **1-800-891-4416**, at any hour.

## Is it stress, anxiety or burnout?

[WHO describes stress](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/stress) as "a state of worry or mental tension caused by a difficult situation", and calls it a normal response that helps us deal with challenges. Burnout is narrower. In its ICD-11 classification, WHO lists burnout as an **occupational phenomenon**, not a medical condition, and says it should be used only for work and "not be applied to describe experiences in other areas of life".

| | Everyday stress | Burnout (WHO, ICD-11) | Anxiety disorder |
|---|---|---|---|
| What sets it off | A specific pressure: an exam, a deadline, a family problem | Long-running workplace stress that "has not been successfully managed" | Worry or fear that is out of proportion, or has no clear trigger |
| What it feels like | Tension, irritability, poor sleep, trouble concentrating | Exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about your job, feeling less effective at work | Persistent worry, restlessness, physical symptoms that interfere with daily life |
| Does it ease? | Usually, once the situation passes | Not on its own; the work situation usually has to change | Often needs professional treatment |
| Who to approach | Self-help first; a doctor if it lingers | Self-help plus changes at work; a doctor if your health suffers | A doctor or mental health professional |

The NHS notes that physical stress symptoms like high blood pressure should settle when the stress does; if they don't, get checked. If the third column sounds familiar, read our guide to [anxiety symptoms and causes](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/anxiety-symptoms-and-causes).

## WHO's five stress skills, step by step

WHO's illustrated guide comes with free audio exercises and is meant for a few minutes' practice a day. Its course version, Self-Help Plus, is based on acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), a form of cognitive behavioural therapy: the aim is not to get rid of stressful thoughts but to stop them pushing you around.

### 1. Grounding

1. **Notice** the thoughts and feelings you're having right now.
2. **Slow down and reconnect with your body.** Press your feet into the floor, stretch, or breathe slowly.
3. **Refocus on the world around you.** Notice what you can see, hear, touch, taste and smell, where you are and what you're doing.

Practise it at calm moments, such as waiting for a bus, so it comes easily when you need it.

### 2. Unhooking

When a thought like "I'm going to fail this paper" grabs you:

1. Notice that a thought or feeling has hooked you.
2. Name it silently: "I'm having the thought that I'll fail" or "Here is a feeling of anger."
3. Bring your attention back to what you're doing and the people around you.

### 3. Acting on your values

Decide what kind of person you want to be under pressure: kind, patient, hard-working, fair. Then pick **one small action** for the coming week that matches that value. In a hard situation, the guide says you can leave if that's possible, or change what you can and accept what you can't while still acting on your values.

### 4. Being kind

Notice harsh self-talk ("I'm useless"), name it as an unkind thought, and answer it the way you would answer a friend. The guide also encourages kindness towards others and leaning on people around you.

### 5. Making room

Instead of fighting a painful feeling, notice it with curiosity, as if it were an object: where is it in your body, what size and shape is it? Name it ("I notice sadness"), then breathe into and around it, and let it come and go like the weather.

### What the evidence says

The same skills form WHO's five-session group course, Self-Help Plus. Its first randomised trial, with 694 South Sudanese refugee women in Uganda (*The Lancet Global Health*, 2020), found lower psychological distress three months later than with enhanced usual care, plus better wellbeing and, marginally, functioning; the researchers say longer-term benefit still needs testing. Two small pilot trials in Turkey, with 124 Turkish and Syrian adults, saw symptoms improve among people using the guide, but they were too small to show a clear difference from a waiting-list group, and a full trial is still needed.

## Everyday habits that WHO and the NHS recommend

- **Keep a routine.** WHO suggests set times for meals, family, exercise, chores and rest.
- **Protect your sleep.** Same bed and wake times, even on Sundays; fewer screens late at night; no heavy meals, caffeine or alcohol near bedtime.
- **Move every day.** WHO's 2020 physical activity guidelines say activity "can also reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety" and recommend 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week for adults. That's about 30 minutes of brisk walking on five days.
- **Try slow breathing.** The NHS method: breathe in gently through your nose for a count of up to five, out through your mouth for up to five, and keep going for at least five minutes.
- **Break big tasks into small steps** and plan ahead for hard days, as the NHS suggests.
- **Cut back on news and social media** if they're making you more stressed.
- **Talk to someone you trust.**
- **Avoid alcohol, smoking and other substances as a way to cope.** WHO warns that chronic stress can push up their use, and the NHS says they make things worse.

## Stress in Indian settings

### Exam season

Board exams, JEE, NEET and semester exams arrive on a known calendar, so learn these skills before February, not during it. The Ministry of Education's **Manodarpan** initiative runs a national toll-free tele-counselling line for school, college and university students on **8448440632**.

### Long working hours

WHO and the ILO estimated that working **55 hours or more a week** carries about a 35% higher risk of stroke and a 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease than a 35–40 hour week. They estimated 745,000 such deaths worldwide in 2016, with a particularly heavy burden in the South-East Asia region (which includes India) and the Western Pacific. India's Economic Survey 2024-25 also flagged studies linking work beyond 55–60 hours a week to poorer health. If your job routinely demands this much, breathing exercises won't make up for it; the hours need to change.

### Why getting help early matters

India's National Mental Health Survey 2015-16, carried out by NIMHANS with 34,802 adults across 12 states, found that 10.6% of adults had a current mental disorder. The treatment gap ranged from 28% to 83% depending on the condition. In other words, many people who need help never get it, so don't wait for things to become severe.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor (a general physician is a fine first step) or a mental health professional if:

- stress symptoms last for weeks or keep coming back after the pressure has passed
- you can't work, study, sleep or look after yourself properly
- you're relying on alcohol, tobacco or other substances to get through the day
- you have chest pain, a racing heart or other physical symptoms that worry you
- you feel low, hopeless or lose interest in things you used to enjoy (read about the [symptoms of depression](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/depression-symptoms-causes))
- you get sudden waves of intense fear with a pounding heart (see [panic attacks](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/panic-attacks-symptoms-causes))

**Get help right away** if you feel unable to keep yourself safe. Call **112** or go to the nearest hospital emergency department. Tele-MANAS (**14416** or **1-800-891-4416**) is free, open 24 hours a day and, according to the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, available in 20 Indian languages.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the fastest way to calm down when stressed?

Grounding from WHO's guide takes under a minute: notice what you're feeling, press your feet into the floor and breathe slowly, then notice what you can see, hear and touch around you.

### Is burnout a medical diagnosis?

No. WHO's ICD-11 lists burnout as an occupational phenomenon caused by chronic workplace stress that hasn't been managed, not as a medical condition. A doctor can still check whether something else is behind your symptoms.

### Is the WHO stress guide free and available in Hindi?

The guide and its audio exercises are free on WHO's website. WHO lists the guide in more than 40 languages, including Hindi, Bengali, Nepali and Urdu. The audio exercises are not offered in every language, and we found none in Hindi.

### Is Tele-MANAS really free?

Yes. It is a toll-free Government of India service launched in October 2022. You can call 14416 or 1-800-891-4416 at any hour, and the Ministry of Health says it had taken over 18 lakh calls by February 2025.
