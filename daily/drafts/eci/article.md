**Quick answer:** The Election Commission of India (ECI) runs elections to the Lok Sabha, Rajya Sabha, state Assemblies and the offices of President and Vice-President. Article 324 of the Constitution gives it "superintendence, direction and control" of voter lists and polls, so it fixes schedules, enforces the Model Code of Conduct, supplies EVMs, and registers parties and allots symbols.

Its power has limits. It does not run panchayat or municipal elections, it cannot decide election disputes, and the Supreme Court has said it can deregister a party only in three narrow cases. In 2026 the Supreme Court has been testing both how its members are picked and its Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of voter rolls.

## What Article 324 gives the Commission

The Commission was set up on 25 January 1950, a day before the Constitution came into force (see [why 26 January is Republic Day](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-is-republic-day-celebrated)). Its powers rest on a few provisions:

| Provision | What it does |
|---|---|
| Article 324(1) | Control of electoral rolls and of elections to Parliament, state legislatures, President and Vice-President |
| Article 324(2) | A Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) plus as many Election Commissioners as the President fixes |
| Article 324(5) | The CEC can be removed only like a Supreme Court judge; other commissioners only on the CEC's recommendation |
| Article 326 | Adult suffrage for every citizen aged 18 or above |
| Article 329(b) | Results can be challenged only through an election petition |

The Commission has had three members since 1993. Under the 2023 appointments law, it acts unanimously as far as possible and by majority otherwise; the CEC has no veto.

Its routine work includes fixing election phases, recognising parties and allotting symbols under the Election Symbols Order, 1968 (including after a split), watching candidates' spending (₹95 lakh for a Lok Sabha seat in larger states and ₹40 lakh for an Assembly seat, set in January 2022), and advising the President or Governor on disqualification of MPs and MLAs under Articles 103 and 192. It must act within the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951; Article 324 fills gaps only where those laws are silent.

## How commissioners are appointed

For decades the government alone chose commissioners. On 2 March 2023, a five-judge Constitution Bench in **Anoop Baranwal v Union of India** ruled that, until Parliament made a law, a committee of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India should advise on appointments.

Parliament then passed the **Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023**, signed into law on 28 December 2023 and in force from 2 January 2024. According to PRS Legislative Research and reports of the debate:

- **Selection committee:** the Prime Minister, a Cabinet Minister he nominates, and the Leader of the Opposition (or leader of the largest opposition party). The Chief Justice is not on it.
- **Search committee:** headed by the Law Minister, it shortlists five names; the selection committee may look beyond the list.
- **Term:** six years or age 65, whichever comes first; candidates must be or have been Secretary-level officers.
- **Section 16:** bars civil or criminal cases against current or former commissioners for acts done in their official duties.

**The government's case:** the Law Minister told the Rajya Sabha the Bill had a limited purpose, Parliament was filling the gap the judgment itself left, and the Leader of the Opposition's seat was a check.

**The critics' case:** petitioners and PRS note that the government holds two of three votes on the committee, which they argue weakens the Commission's independence.

### Status of the court challenge (as of 22 November 2026)

The Supreme Court refused to stay the law in January and March 2024, so appointments since then have been made under it. According to the Supreme Court Observer, a two-judge bench heard the case in May 2026, split on 23 September on whether a larger bench was needed, and sent it to the Chief Justice to set up a Constitution Bench. On 20 November, Deccan Herald reported, the court declined to hear a plea to recall that reference separately, saying it could be raised before the Constitution Bench. A separate challenge to Section 16 has been pending since January 2026, with no stay.

## The Model Code of Conduct: rules without a statute

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) bars parties, candidates and governments from, for example, announcing new schemes to sway voters, using official machinery for campaigns or appealing to caste or religion. It applies from the day the schedule is announced until results, a point the Supreme Court settled in Union of India v Harbans Singh Jalal.

The MCC is **not a law**. The Commission enforces it with notices, censure, short campaign bans and transfers of officials. Only breaches that are also offences, such as bribery, can be prosecuted under the 1951 Act or criminal law.

A parliamentary committee recommended legal backing in 2013; the Commission opposed it, saying courts would be too slow during a campaign. Critics writing in Bar and Bench say enforcement is uneven and rarely deters.

## EVMs and VVPAT: what the Supreme Court ruled

On 26 April 2024, in **Association for Democratic Reforms v Election Commission of India**, the Supreme Court rejected pleas for paper ballots or counting 100% of VVPAT slips, saying paper ballots would bring back problems like booth capturing. It added two safeguards, as summarised by the Supreme Court Observer:

1. Symbol Loading Units must be sealed and kept for 45 days after results.
2. The candidate placed second or third can ask, within seven days, for engineers to check the burnt memory of up to 5% of EVMs in a constituency, at their own cost, refunded if tampering is found.

These add to the 2019 rule that VVPAT slips are counted for five random EVMs per Assembly constituency or segment.

## Voter rolls: summary revision and the SIR

Rolls are normally updated by **summary revision**: a draft roll, claims and objections, then a final roll. Since 2022 you can enrol against four qualifying dates a year (1 January, 1 April, 1 July, 1 October), and 17-year-olds can apply in advance, according to PIB.

An **intensive revision** rebuilds the roll through house-to-house enumeration. In June 2025 the Commission began a Special Intensive Revision in Bihar, the first there since 2003, saying rolls had to be cleaned of dead, shifted, duplicate and ineligible names. Petitioners such as the Association for Democratic Reforms argued it put the burden of proof on voters and could disenfranchise eligible citizens.

On 27 May 2026, a bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant **upheld the Bihar SIR**, according to LiveLaw and India Legal. It held that:

- the exercise is valid under Section 21(3) of the 1950 Act read with Article 324;
- the Commission may examine citizenship only to decide roll eligibility, and its view is not a final ruling on citizenship;
- doubtful cases go to the authority under the Citizenship Act, and wrongly deleted voters keep their appeal rights.

About 47 lakh names were removed in Bihar, according to the Supreme Court Observer. After Phase II (12 more states and UTs), on 14 May 2026 the Commission announced Phase III in 16 states and three UTs, about 36.73 crore electors (All India Radio). State-level challenges continue. In Delhi, the court suggested in October that voters who moved within the city be allowed to use Form 8 (change of address) rather than Form 6, and on 19 November it said it would review progress on 2 December before deciding whether to extend the 30 November deadline.

## What the Election Commission cannot do

- **Run local body polls.** Panchayat and municipal elections belong to each state's State Election Commission (Articles 243K and 243ZA).
- **Decide election disputes.** These go to the High Court as election petitions.
- **Decide defections.** Under the anti-defection law, the Speaker or Chairman decides.
- **Freely deregister parties.** A 2002 Supreme Court judgment allows cancellation only if registration was got by fraud, the party drops allegiance to the Constitution, or the Centre declares it unlawful. The Commission has sought wider powers since at least 2016. In 2025 it "delisted" 808 registered unrecognised parties that had not contested for six years (PIB); in November 2025 the Kerala High Court stayed that for some petitioners, questioning whether the law allows it.
- **Make election law.** Spending limits and qualifying dates are set by Parliament and the Union government, often on the Commission's advice.

You can seek the Commission's records under the RTI Act ([how to file an RTI online](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-file-rti-online)). Official orders are on the [Election Commission's website](https://www.eci.gov.in/), and PRS has a [brief on the 2023 appointments law](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/prs-products/prs-legislative-brief-4256).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many members does the Election Commission of India have?

Three: the Chief Election Commissioner and two Election Commissioners, as it has been since 1993.

### Who conducts panchayat and municipal elections?

Each state's State Election Commission, under Articles 243K and 243ZA, not the ECI.

### Is the Model Code of Conduct legally binding?

No. It is a set of guidelines enforced through the Commission's Article 324 authority. Only breaches that are also offences under election or criminal law can be prosecuted.

### Did the Supreme Court strike down the SIR?

No. On 27 May 2026 it upheld the Bihar SIR under Section 21(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1950 and Article 324, while saying the Commission's view on citizenship is not final. Challenges in other states are still being heard.
