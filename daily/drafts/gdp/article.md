**Quick answer:** GDP (gross domestic product) is the market value of all final goods and services produced inside India in a period, usually a quarter or a financial year (April to March). It is calculated by the National Statistics Office (NSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), mainly by adding up the value added by every sector of the economy and then adding product taxes and subtracting product subsidies.

India's latest full-year figure, from MoSPI's Provisional Estimates released on 5 June 2026, puts GDP for 2025-26 at ₹346.36 lakh crore at current prices and ₹323.12 lakh crore at constant (2022-23) prices, a real growth of 7.7%. These numbers come from a new series with base year 2022-23, released on 27 February 2026, which replaced the old 2011-12 series.

## GDP and GVA: what is the difference?

Two headline numbers come out in every MoSPI release, and they are not the same thing.

- **GVA (gross value added)** is what producers add: the value of their output minus the inputs they bought. A bakery that sells bread for ₹100 after buying ₹60 of flour, fuel and packaging adds ₹40.
- **GDP** is GVA plus taxes on products (GST, excise and so on) minus subsidies on products.

So, roughly: **GDP = GVA + product taxes − product subsidies.**

For 2025-26, MoSPI puts nominal GVA at ₹314.87 lakh crore against GDP of ₹346.36 lakh crore; the gap of about ₹31.5 lakh crore is net product taxes. GVA is the better guide to how each sector is doing. GDP is used for international comparisons and ratios like debt-to-GDP.

## Three ways to measure the same economy

Everything produced is either spent by someone or earned as income by someone, so in theory three methods give the same answer.

| Approach | What is added up | How India uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Production | Value added by each industry (GVA), plus net product taxes | The main method for India's headline GDP |
| Expenditure | Household spending + government spending + investment + exports − imports | Published alongside; shows who is buying |
| Income | Wages + profits + rent + interest + mixed income of the self-employed | Used in parts of the accounts; harder to measure where many people are self-employed |

In practice the production and expenditure totals never match exactly, so MoSPI shows the gap as a line called "discrepancies". The new series uses Supply and Use Tables to reconcile the two sides, according to MoSPI's FAQ. On the spending side, private consumption was 56.7% of GDP in 2025-26 and fixed investment 31.9%, at current prices.

## Who publishes India's GDP and when

The NSO's National Accounts Division compiles the estimates from company filings (MCA-21), GST data, the Index of Industrial Production, crop estimates, government accounts, and household surveys such as the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) and the Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises (ASUSE).

A single year's GDP is published several times as better data arrives. Based on MoSPI's Advance Release Calendar, here is the cycle for 2026-27:

| Estimate | For year 2026-27 | Based on |
|---|---|---|
| First Advance Estimates | 7 January 2027 | About 7–8 months of data, projected forward |
| Second Advance Estimates | 26 February 2027 | Adds data for October–December |
| Provisional Estimates | Early June 2027 | The full year, with Q4 |
| First Revised Estimates | February 2028 (expected) | Fuller company accounts and survey data |
| Final Estimates | February 2029 (expected) | The most complete data |

From 2026, MoSPI moved the Provisional Estimates release from the last working day of May to 7 June (or the working day before it), according to a MoSPI press note. That is why the 2025-26 figures came out on 5 June 2026.

### Quarterly GDP

MoSPI also publishes GDP for each quarter, about two months after the quarter ends. The most recent release, the press note for Q1 (April–June) 2026-27 dated 31 August 2026, showed:

- real GDP growth of 7.8% (₹81.36 lakh crore), up from 6.9% a year earlier
- real GVA growth of 8.2%
- nominal GDP growth of 10.3% (₹88.27 lakh crore)

The Q2 (July–September) estimates are scheduled for 30 November 2026. Quarterly figures take last year's level and move it with fast-arriving indicators, following the IMF's Quarterly National Accounts Manual, so they get revised often.

## Why GDP numbers change after they are published

Revisions are normal everywhere, but India's 2026 changes were unusually large because of the base-year switch. Take GDP for April–June 2025, at current prices:

| Release | GDP for Q1 2025-26 (current prices) |
|---|---|
| Old 2011-12 series, August 2025 | ₹86.05 lakh crore |
| New 2022-23 series, February 2026 | ₹80.32 lakh crore |
| Provisional Estimates, June 2026 | ₹80.44 lakh crore |
| After new IIP and producer price data, August 2026 | ₹80.00 lakh crore |

Some critics argued that last year's figure had been lowered to make this year's growth look faster. MoSPI rejected this in a clarification on 2 September 2026, saying the difference comes from the new base year, data and methods, and that growth must be compared within one series, not against the superseded ₹86.05 lakh crore.

Annual growth also moved. In the old series, real growth for 2023-24 had been put at 9.2% and for 2024-25 at 6.5%. The new series puts them at 7.2% and 7.1%.

## The new base year: what changed in 2026

The base year is the year whose prices are used to measure real growth. The previous update was in 2015 (to 2011-12). According to MoSPI's FAQ, its advisory committee chose 2022-23 because 2017-18 to 2021-22 were disturbed by the GST rollout, COVID-19 and the rebound after it. The main changes:

- **Informal businesses** are measured with annual ASUSE and PLFS survey data instead of being projected forward from an old base year. Paid domestic workers and gig work such as delivery are covered more directly.
- **Double deflation** in manufacturing and agriculture: output and inputs are adjusted for their own price changes separately, using wholesale prices at first and, since August 2026, the new Producer Price Indices.
- **More administrative data**: GST, e-Vahan vehicle data, and actual central government spending from the Public Financial Management System.

MoSPI published a "Sources and Methods" handbook for the new series in September 2026. A back series recalculating older years to 1950-51 is expected by December 2026, according to the FAQ. Until then, long comparisons across the two series are not like-for-like.

## Nominal vs real GDP: a worked example

Nominal GDP uses each year's actual prices. Real GDP values everything at base-year prices, so it shows only the change in quantity.

Imagine an economy that makes only rice and phones:

| | Year 1 (base year) | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | 100 kg at ₹50 = ₹5,000 | 110 kg at ₹55 = ₹6,050 |
| Phones | 10 at ₹10,000 = ₹1,00,000 | 11 at ₹10,500 = ₹1,15,500 |
| Nominal GDP | ₹1,05,000 | ₹1,21,550 |
| Real GDP (Year 1 prices) | ₹1,05,000 | ₹1,15,500 |

Nominal GDP grew 15.8%, but real GDP grew 10%. The rest is price rise. Dividing nominal by real (₹1,21,550 ÷ ₹1,15,500) gives the **GDP deflator** of about 1.052, meaning prices across this economy rose about 5.2%. This is why [printing more money doesn't make a country richer](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-cant-we-print-more-money): it lifts nominal GDP, not real output.

In India's Q1 2026-27 data, nominal growth was 10.3% and real growth 7.8%. Unlike the consumer price index, which tracks a household shopping basket (see [how inflation is measured in India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-is-inflation-measured-in-india)), the GDP deflator covers everything produced, including machinery, exports and services.

## The main debates about how India measures GDP

**The informal sector.** Much Indian work happens in small unregistered businesses that file no accounts. In its November 2025 review, the IMF gave India's national accounts a "C" grade, its second-lowest, citing the old 2011-12 base, sizeable gaps between production and expenditure estimates, and weak informal-sector coverage. MoSPI's answer was the new series, which measures these businesses with annual surveys. In September 2026 an IMF spokesperson said the new industrial production and producer price series "should help improve India's GDP estimates", while encouraging further work on data quality.

**Deflators.** The IMF also said India relied too much on wholesale prices and "single deflation", which can distort real growth when input and output prices move differently. The new double-deflation method produced a surprise in Q1 2026-27: manufacturing's nominal GVA rose 7.7% but real GVA rose 9.2%, a deflator of −1.5%. Critics asked how that was possible when prices were rising. MoSPI explained that when input prices rise faster than output prices, as in textiles, basic metals and rubber and plastic products, real value added can grow faster than nominal value added.

**Discrepancies.** MoSPI says the "discrepancies" line is a balancing item, not proof that GDP is over- or under-stated, and expects it to be close to zero in final estimates, as it was for 2022-23 and 2023-24.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who calculates India's GDP?

The National Statistics Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) compiles and releases India's GDP estimates. The Reserve Bank of India and the IMF publish forecasts, but the official figures come from MoSPI.

### What is India's GDP per person?

According to MoSPI's June 2026 release, per capita GDP at current prices for 2025-26 was ₹2,43,803, and per capita net national income was ₹2,08,090. Our explainer on [per capita income in India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/per-capita-income-india) covers what that average hides.

### Why is GVA growth different from GDP growth?

GDP adds product taxes and subtracts subsidies; GVA does not. If taxes grow faster than output, or subsidies are cut, GDP grows faster than GVA, and vice versa.
