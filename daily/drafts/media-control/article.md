**Quick answer:** Controlling the news media is one of the clearest signs of dictatorship, but not every rule about the media is control. Democracies regulate the press too, through defamation law, broadcast licences and limits on incitement. The line is crossed when the state decides *what* may be reported, punishes critics, or quietly takes over ownership so that only one view reaches the public.

Nazi Germany, the Soviet Union and, more recently, Hungary and Russia each silenced independent journalism with a mix of law, money and fear. India's Constitution and Supreme Court have marked where legitimate regulation ends, and India itself lived through 21 months of press censorship during the Emergency of 1975–77.

## How authoritarian governments control the news

Research by International Media Support and the Journal of Democracy describes the same few levers:

- **Censorship:** officials approve stories before publication, or ban topics and words.
- **Ownership:** the state, or friendly businesspeople, buy up outlets ("proxy-owned" media, in the Journal of Democracy's term) that keep a look of independence.
- **Licences:** permission to broadcast is refused or not renewed.
- **Money:** government advertising, newsprint and tax are used as rewards or punishments.
- **Intimidation and lawfare:** arrests, raids, defamation suits and vague "fake news" or security laws breed self-censorship.

## Regulation or control? The difference

| Question to ask | Legitimate regulation | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets the rule? | Parliament, in a law open to challenge | The executive, often by order or informal pressure |
| What does it target? | Specific harms: incitement, defamation, contempt of court, obscenity | Criticism of the government, or whole topics |
| Is it applied equally? | Same rule for friendly and critical outlets | Critics punished, friends spared |
| Can a court review it? | Yes, with reasons given in public | Orders are secret or not open to appeal |
| Is it proportionate? | Least restrictive step, for a limited time | Indefinite bans, shutdowns, closures |
| Who owns the media? | Ownership is diverse and disclosed | Ownership is concentrated with allies of the state |

## What India's Constitution says

Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression. There is no separate "press freedom" clause, but in Romesh Thappar (1950) the Supreme Court said "freedom of speech and of the press lay at the foundation of all democratic organisations". Article 19(2) allows only "reasonable restrictions" on eight grounds: sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence. Criticising the government is not one of them. (The Constitution took effect on 26 January 1950; see [why Republic Day is celebrated](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-is-republic-day-celebrated).)

## What the Supreme Court has said

Case summaries are from Columbia University's Global Freedom of Expression database.

- **Romesh Thappar v State of Madras (26 May 1950):** a ban on the journal *Cross Roads* entering Madras was struck down. The First Amendment (1951) then added "public order" to Article 19(2).
- **Sakal Papers v Union of India (1961):** an order capping the number of pages a newspaper could print for its price was struck down because it restricted circulation.
- **Bennett Coleman v Union of India (30 October 1972):** a newsprint policy capping pages was struck down; control of newsprint cannot become control of content.
- **Indian Express Newspapers v Union of India (6 December 1984, reported 1985):** newspapers can be taxed, but not so heavily that expression is stifled. The Court asked the government to re-examine newsprint import duty.
- **Shreya Singhal v Union of India (24 March 2015):** Section 66A of the IT Act was struck down. Advocacy, "howsoever unpopular", is protected; only incitement can be punished.
- **Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India (10 January 2020):** expression online is protected; indefinite internet suspension is not allowed, and restrictions must pass a proportionality test.
- **Madhyamam Broadcasting (MediaOne) v Union of India (5 April 2023):** refusal to renew a channel's licence was quashed. "National security claims cannot be made out of thin air," the Court said, and critical views on government policy cannot be called "anti-establishment".

The government argues that regulating misinformation is a legitimate aim; courts check how it is done. In September 2024 the Bombay High Court struck down a 2023 IT Rules amendment letting a government Fact Check Unit flag online content about the Centre's business as false; the original two judges had split in January 2024, and a third judge's opinion decided it. The government had said the unit would serve the public interest by curbing misinformation. In March 2026 the Supreme Court agreed to hear the Centre's appeal but refused to stay the High Court ruling, so the case is pending (LiveLaw).

## India's Emergency: a documented example

According to a Press Information Bureau factsheet, from 26 June 1975 newspapers needed government clearance before publishing news, editorials and photographs. Citing the Shah Commission, it says power to Delhi newspaper offices was cut, papers were labelled "friendly", "neutral" or "hostile", and the Press Council of India was abolished. The Emergency was lifted on 21 March 1977. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for emergency and, under Article 352(3), required the Cabinet's written advice.

## Where India stands on press freedom indices

| Index | Latest result for India | Previous |
|---|---|---|
| RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026 (30 April 2026) | 157th of 180, score 31.96, "very serious" | 151st, score 32.96 (2025) |
| Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026 | 62/100, "Partly Free"; 2 of 4 on free and independent media | 63/100 (2025) |

On its [India page](https://rsf.org/en/country/india), RSF says media ownership "has become heavily concentrated in the hands of a few conglomerates that are mostly allied with the government", and Scroll reported that it flagged growing use of defamation and national security laws against journalists. Freedom House says segments of private media remain vigorous and diverse and still scrutinise politicians, but journalists face harassment and legal action.

**The government's view:** In a written Lok Sabha reply (Starred Question 49, 6 February 2024), the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting said it "does not agree" with RSF's conclusions on India, citing a "very low sample size" and "little or no weightage to fundamentals of democracy", and called the methodology questionable and non-transparent ([Lok Sabha answer](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/2974728/1/AS49.pdf)). In March 2021 the ministry called Freedom House's downgrade "misleading, incorrect and misplaced", saying debate and dissent are part of Indian democracy (PIB). Both indices lean partly on expert questionnaires, so treat them as informed assessments, not exact measurements.

## How to spot captured media: a reader's checklist

- **Who owns it?** Check the owner's other businesses, especially any that depend on government contracts or licences.
- **Who pays for it?** Government ads alone prove nothing, but notice whether coverage of the advertiser is ever critical.
- **Does it ever question power?** Has it criticised the government of the day, at the Centre or in your state, on facts? Are critics given space, or dismissed with labels instead of answered?
- **Same story, same words?** Identical headlines across many outlets on one day suggest a common script.
- **Compare sources.** Read one story in two or three papers; our list of [top English newspapers in India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/top-english-newspapers-in-india) is a start.
- **Check viral claims** with PIB Fact Check, Alt News, BOOM or AFP Fact Check.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is controlling the media always a sign of dictatorship?

Controlling content, ownership and critics is a hallmark of dictatorships. Regulating specific harms through laws that courts can review is normal in democracies.

### Does India's Constitution mention freedom of the press?

Not in those words. The Supreme Court has held since 1950 that press freedom is part of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), subject only to Article 19(2).

### What is India's rank in the World Press Freedom Index 2026?

India is 157th of 180 with a score of 31.96, down from 151st in 2025. The Union government has told Parliament it disagrees with the index's conclusions and methodology.

### Was the press censored during the Emergency in India?

Yes. From 26 June 1975 newspapers needed government clearance before publishing, according to a PIB factsheet citing the Shah Commission. Censorship ended with the Emergency on 21 March 1977.
