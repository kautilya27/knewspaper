**Quick answer:** Anaemia (also spelt anemia) means your blood has too little haemoglobin, the iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The usual signs are tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on exertion, headaches, dizziness and pale skin. The most common cause is iron deficiency. Other causes include low vitamin B12 or folate, worm infections, heavy periods, blood loss, long-term illness and inherited blood disorders such as thalassaemia and sickle cell disease.

Anaemia is very common in India. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), 67.1% of children aged 6–59 months and 57% of women aged 15–49 were anaemic. A simple blood test, the complete blood count (CBC), shows whether you have it. Finding the cause takes a doctor and usually more tests.

## What haemoglobin level counts as anaemia?

The World Health Organization (WHO) sets the haemoglobin levels below which a person counts as anaemic. Its updated guideline of March 2024 kept most older cut-offs but lowered two: for children aged 6–23 months and for the second trimester of pregnancy.

Indian lab reports usually show haemoglobin in g/dL, so the table uses that unit (WHO writes g/L; 110 g/L equals 11.0 g/dL).

| Group | Anaemia if haemoglobin is below (WHO, 2024) |
|---|---|
| Children, 6–23 months | 10.5 g/dL |
| Children, 24–59 months | 11.0 g/dL |
| Children, 5–11 years | 11.5 g/dL |
| Children, 12–14 years | 12.0 g/dL |
| Non-pregnant women, 15–65 years | 12.0 g/dL |
| Men, 15–65 years | 13.0 g/dL |
| Pregnant women, 1st trimester | 11.0 g/dL |
| Pregnant women, 2nd trimester | 10.5 g/dL |
| Pregnant women, 3rd trimester | 11.0 g/dL |

These are sea-level cut-offs. WHO's 2024 guideline adjusts them for people living 500 metres or more above sea level, which covers hill areas such as Ladakh, Sikkim, Himachal and Uttarakhand as well as some plateau cities. They are also adjusted for smokers. WHO grades anaemia as mild, moderate or severe, so let your doctor interpret your report rather than going by the number alone.

## Symptoms of anaemia

Mild anaemia often causes no symptoms and turns up only in a routine test or pregnancy check-up. When symptoms do appear, they come on slowly and are easy to blame on stress.

### Common symptoms

- Tiredness and low energy, even after rest
- Weakness, and getting breathless on stairs or a short walk
- Headaches and dizziness
- Pale skin, or pale inner eyelids, lips and nails
- Cold hands and feet
- A racing or pounding heartbeat (palpitations)

### Signs that point to iron deficiency

The NHS lists a few less common signs of iron deficiency anaemia. They include a sore tongue, painful sores at the corners of the mouth, hair loss, spoon-shaped nails, and cravings for non-food items such as ice or paper (called pica).

### Signs that point to vitamin B12 deficiency

Low B12 can also affect the nerves, causing numbness or pins and needles, muscle weakness, balance problems, memory trouble and mood changes. According to the NHS, some of this damage can become permanent if not treated early.

## What causes anaemia?

WHO describes anaemia as a sign of an underlying problem, not a disease in itself, so the cause matters.

| Cause | How it leads to anaemia | Who is more at risk in India |
|---|---|---|
| Iron deficiency | Too little iron to make haemoglobin, from a low-iron diet, poor absorption or blood loss | Young children, adolescent girls, women, pregnant women |
| Vitamin B12 deficiency | B12 is needed to make healthy red cells; it comes mainly from animal foods | Strict vegetarians and vegans, older adults, people on some long-term medicines |
| Folate deficiency | The body stores folate for only a few months, so a poor diet shows up quickly | Pregnant women, people with a poor diet, heavy drinkers |
| Worm infections | Hookworm and other worms cause blood and nutrient loss from the gut | Children, people without safe sanitation or who walk barefoot on soil |
| Heavy periods and other blood loss | Iron is lost faster than the diet replaces it; bleeding from piles or stomach ulcers can do the same | Women and adolescent girls; people with gut problems |
| Chronic illness and infections | Long-term inflammation, kidney disease, TB, HIV or malaria reduce red cell production or destroy cells | People with long-term conditions |
| Inherited blood disorders | Thalassaemia and sickle cell disease change how haemoglobin is made | Families with a history of these disorders; sickle cell is more common in some tribal communities |

### A note for vegetarians

Iron from plant foods is absorbed less easily than iron from meat, and vitamin B12 comes mainly from animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy. The NHS notes that people who don't regularly eat these foods can become deficient, and vegans are especially at risk. B12 stores can last two to five years, so deficiency builds up quietly. If you're vegetarian and always tired, ask your doctor whether B12 should be checked along with haemoglobin.

### Thalassaemia and sickle cell disease

These are inherited through genes. They are not caused by diet and are not contagious. Thalassaemia trait is often mistaken for iron deficiency because both show small red cells on a CBC. That is one reason doctors test before they treat.

India launched the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission on 1 July 2023 from Shahdol in Madhya Pradesh. Its first phase aimed to screen about 7 crore people aged up to 40 in 17 high-focus states between 2023-24 and 2025-26, and the government's goal is to eliminate sickle cell disease as a public health problem by 2047.

## What is Anaemia Mukt Bharat?

Anaemia Mukt Bharat (AMB) is the Health Ministry's programme to reduce anaemia, launched in 2018. On 29 June 2026 the Ministry issued new operational guidelines for what it now calls Anemia Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan, replacing the original "6x6x6" strategy with a "7x7x7" one: seven target groups, seven interventions and seven institutional mechanisms.

The seven groups are low birth weight babies aged 0–6 months, children aged 6–59 months, children aged 5–9 years, adolescents aged 10–19, pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers and women of reproductive age (20–49). The interventions include preventive iron and folic acid supplementation, anaemia testing and treatment, deworming, delayed cord clamping at birth, community awareness, tackling non-nutritional causes such as malaria and inherited blood disorders, and promoting locally available iron-rich foods. The programme's "Test, Treat, Talk" approach now adds a fourth step, "Track", to follow people up after treatment or referral. Through AMB, your local Anganwadi, school health programme or government health centre may offer free haemoglobin testing.

## Food and anaemia: what the guidelines say

Food alone won't fix anaemia caused by B12 deficiency, bleeding or an inherited disorder, but a balanced diet helps prevent iron deficiency. WHO suggests:

- Eating iron-rich foods such as pulses and legumes, dark green leafy vegetables, fortified cereals and, for non-vegetarians, meat, fish and poultry. In an Indian kitchen, think dal, rajma, chana, palak and methi.
- Pairing them with vitamin C-rich fruits and vegetables, which help the body absorb iron, such as amla, guava, oranges or a squeeze of lemon.
- Not drinking tea or coffee with iron-rich meals, because they reduce iron absorption.

The ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition's Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) advise avoiding tea and coffee for at least an hour before and after meals. That's worth knowing when chai so often comes with breakfast. For building a balanced plate, see our guide to [a well-balanced diet](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/the-importance-of-a-well-balanced-diet) and our list of [protein-rich vegetables](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/which-vegetables-can-beat-egg-in-protein) for vegetarian meals.

Don't start iron or vitamin tablets on your own. If your anaemia has another cause, self-treatment can delay the right diagnosis, and too much iron can be harmful, especially for people with thalassaemia.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor and ask for a CBC if you:

- Feel tired, weak or breathless for weeks without a clear reason
- Have very heavy or long periods
- Are pregnant or planning a pregnancy (haemoglobin is checked at antenatal visits)
- Notice pins and needles, numbness or balance problems
- Have a family history of thalassaemia or sickle cell disease
- Have a child who looks pale, eats poorly, or eats mud or chalk

**Get medical help urgently** if you have breathlessness at rest, chest pain, a very fast heartbeat, fainting, black or tarry stools, blood in your vomit or stools, or very pale skin with confusion. These can mean severe anaemia or active bleeding.

Based on your CBC, the doctor may order more tests, such as ferritin (iron stores), B12 and folate levels, a stool test, or haemoglobin electrophoresis for thalassaemia and sickle cell. Treatment depends on the cause, and the doctor decides it only after these tests.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it spelt anaemia or anemia?

Both are correct. "Anaemia" is the British spelling used by WHO and many Indian government documents, while "anemia" is the American spelling. They mean the same condition.

### What haemoglobin level counts as anaemia in women?

WHO's cut-off is 12.0 g/dL for non-pregnant women aged 15–65, so a lower value counts as anaemia. During pregnancy, the WHO cut-off is 11.0 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and 10.5 g/dL in the second. Cut-offs are adjusted for people living at altitude.

### Can anaemia go away on its own?

Usually the cause has to be found and treated first. Anaemia from bleeding, B12 deficiency or an inherited disorder won't go away just by waiting, so get tested.

### Which test shows anaemia?

A complete blood count (CBC) shows your haemoglobin level and the size of your red cells. Your doctor may then order follow-up tests, such as ferritin, B12 or haemoglobin electrophoresis, to find out why you are anaemic.
