This is an opinion piece. It argues about a practice, not a person, and every fact is sourced.

**Quick answer:** Not entirely. A leader still answers to Parliament, the courts, RTI and voters. But a leader who never takes open press questions gives up the one tool that tests a leader in real time, with follow-ups, on the public record. No Indian Prime Minister has held an open press conference in India since 3 January 2014. Most large democracies have routines that make open questioning a habit, and India would be better served by one too.

## What a press conference does that nothing else does

- **Unscripted questions.** Many outlets ask, so the leader cannot pick only friendly ones.
- **Follow-ups.** If one reporter is dodged, the next can ask again. That is how evasion becomes visible.
- **A public record.** Answers are given in front of everyone and can be quoted back later.

Interviews, rallies and radio addresses do at most one of these.

## How other democracies do it

| Country | How the leader or their office faces open questions | Source |
|---|---|---|
| United States | President Obama held 163 news conferences in 8 years; President Biden 37 in 4 years; the current President 19 in his second term up to 20 September 2026. The press secretary also briefs reporters regularly | American Presidency Project |
| United Kingdom | Prime Minister's Questions every sitting Wednesday, 12 noon to 12.30 pm. The PM's spokesman briefs Lobby journalists; No 10 dropped the afternoon briefing from 2026, which the Lobby criticised | UK Parliament; Press Gazette |
| Germany | The Bundespressekonferenz, a journalists' association founded in 1949, invites government spokespeople three times a week, and "anyone can ask a question". The Chancellor's summer press conference was held there on 15 July 2026 | German Federal Government |
| Japan | The Chief Cabinet Secretary holds two news conferences each weekday; the Prime Minister holds press conferences at key moments and takes reporters' questions | Japan Times |
| France | The President's wide-ranging Élysée press conferences are rare; AP noted "few" before the one in January 2024 | AP via PBS |

The US counts are from the [American Presidency Project](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/data/newsconferences.php); the PMQs timing is from [the UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/how/business/questions/). Frequency varies, but where a leader rarely meets the press, there is usually another routine for open questioning: a spokesperson's daily briefing or a weekly slot in Parliament.

## India's own record

India once had this habit. Jawaharlal Nehru's meetings with the press were known as "Monthly Press Conferences", and Rajiv Gandhi gave a live televised press conference on 7 July 1985, taking more than 30 questions, according to a 2024 column in *The Hans India*.

It was never steady. The same column says P.V. Narasimha Rao avoided formal press conferences for most of his term, and Atal Bihari Vajpayee held none in Delhi in six years, though he met reporters informally. Manmohan Singh revived the practice in 2004. At his last press conference, on 3 January 2014, he took more than 62 questions from about 100 journalists, *The Wire* reported, citing his former adviser Pankaj Pachauri.

Since then, reputable reporting documents:

- On 17 May 2019, the current Prime Minister appeared and spoke at a press conference at his party's headquarters but passed questions to the party president (*Business Standard*).
- In RTI replies in 2019 and 2020, the PMO said his media interactions are "structured and unstructured" and no record is kept (*Business Standard*/IANS; Logically Facts).
- He took questions at a joint press conference at the White House on 22 June 2023 (NPR).
- He gave 80 media interviews during the 2024 Lok Sabha campaign, according to PTI.
- At a joint press meet in Oslo on 18 May 2026, no questions were taken; a Norwegian reporter asked why and got no reply (*Al Jazeera*).

**The government's side.** In a May 2024 interview with Aaj Tak, the Prime Minister said, "I have never refused interviews", that "today's media is not what it used to be", and that he is "ready to answer any questions" in Parliament (*Business Standard*). In July 2026, a senior Ministry of External Affairs official said in Auckland that the Prime Minister has "perfected the art of direct contact with his electorate", and that Indian voters, mostly rural, "don't like being spoken to through intermediaries" (*Newslaundry*). The Editors Guild of India called this "deeply flawed", saying "scripted, one-way communications" are "not a substitute".

## The counter-arguments, answered honestly

**1. "Parliament is where a Prime Minister is accountable."** The strongest argument, and constitutionally right: under the Constitution in force since [Republic Day 1950](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-is-republic-day-celebrated), the government answers to the Lok Sabha. But there is no weekly slot for questions to the Prime Minister; a former Lok Sabha Secretariat official, Devender Singh, proposed one in a 2025 book (PTI). And Parliament sits for only part of the year. Vital, but not daily.

**2. "He gives interviews."** True, and 80 in one campaign is a lot. But the leader's office chooses the outlet, and no second reporter can press a dodged point. Interviews add to press conferences; they don't replace them.

**3. "Rallies, Mann Ki Baat and social media reach people directly."** They do. Mann Ki Baat has run almost every month since October 2014 and passed 138 episodes by October 2026, according to All India Radio. But accountability means answering questions, not only being heard.

**4. "Ministers and PIB brief the media."** This has real force. Japan's leaders rely heavily on the Chief Cabinet Secretary, and US presidents on the press secretary. Ministers' and PIB briefings do part of this job in India. But some questions only the head of government can answer.

**5. "RTI and the courts are enough."** They matter, but they are slow, and RTI itself shows the gap: the PMO says it keeps no record of the Prime Minister's media interactions.

**6. "Elections are the final verdict."** Yes, and voters have re-elected this government more than once. But elections come every five years; decisions are made in between.

**7. "Press conferences can be staged."** Fair. Questions can be planted or soft, and some established democracies hold them rarely, as the France and Biden figures show. But a staged event is still more open than none, and the fix for bad press conferences is better rules, as in Germany, where journalists run the room.

## What would help

No new law is needed:

1. One open press conference in India each year, plus questions after major foreign visits, as earlier Prime Ministers often did.
2. A weekly slot in Parliament for questions to the Prime Minister while the House sits.
3. A daily on-record briefing by a named government spokesperson, open to all accredited journalists.

A free press is one of the checks a democracy relies on, as our explainer on [how governments can control the media](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/media-control-and-dictatorship) sets out. Taking open questions is the simplest way for any leader, of any party, to show that check is working.

## Frequently asked questions

### When did an Indian Prime Minister last hold a press conference in India?

On 3 January 2014, when Manmohan Singh took more than 62 questions from about 100 journalists, *The Wire* reported. The current Prime Minister has taken questions at some joint press conferences abroad, such as at the White House in June 2023.

### Does the Indian Prime Minister answer questions in Parliament?

The Prime Minister speaks in debates and has said he is ready to answer questions there. But India has no weekly question slot reserved for the Prime Minister, unlike the UK's Prime Minister's Questions.

### What reason has the government given?

The Prime Minister has said he has never refused interviews and that the media has changed. A senior MEA official said in July 2026 that he prefers direct contact with voters.

### Do other democracies require leaders to hold press conferences?

Usually not by law; it is a convention. The US, UK, Germany and Japan have regular routines, though how often the leader personally takes questions varies.
