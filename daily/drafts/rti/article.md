**Quick answer:** The Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005 lets any Indian citizen ask a government office for records it holds, without giving a reason. For central ministries and bodies you file online at rtionline.gov.in with a ₹10 fee (nothing if you are below the poverty line), and the office must reply within 30 days.

If no reply comes, the law treats it as a refusal. You then file a free first appeal within 30 days, and a second appeal to the Information Commission within 90 days.

## What the RTI Act gives you

Section 2(f) defines "information" widely: records, documents, memos, emails, opinions, advice, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, samples and electronic data. It also covers information about a private body that a public authority can obtain under another law, so you can ask the RBI about a bank, for instance. You can ask for copies, certified copies or to inspect files.

- **No reason needed.** Section 6(2) bars the office from asking why you want the information.
- **Late means free.** Under Section 7(6), information supplied after the deadline must be given free of charge.
- **Penalties.** Under Section 20, the Information Commission can fine an officer ₹250 a day, up to ₹25,000, for refusing or delaying without reasonable cause.

## Who must answer, and who is exempt

Every "public authority" must answer: central and state ministries and departments, municipal bodies, panchayats, public sector undertakings and banks, government universities and bodies substantially funded by government. Each names a **Public Information Officer (PIO)**, called a CPIO in central offices. Section 5(2) also requires an **Assistant PIO** at sub-division or sub-district level to receive applications and pass them on.

**Section 24** keeps the intelligence and security organisations listed in the Act's Second Schedule, such as the Intelligence Bureau and R&AW, outside the Act, with two exceptions. Information about **allegations of corruption** must still be given. Information about **allegations of human rights violations** is given only with the Information Commission's approval, within 45 days.

## How to file an RTI application online

The central portal, run by the Department of Personnel and Training (DoPT), covers only central government bodies. Its guidelines warn that an application meant for a state body, including the Delhi government, will be returned **without refund of the fee**.

1. Go to rtionline.gov.in, click **Submit Request**, read the guidelines and tick the box. An account is optional.
2. Choose the ministry or department from the list.
3. Enter your name, address and email; a mobile number gets you SMS alerts.
4. Type your questions. The portal's user manual allows 3,000 characters in the box; put anything longer in a PDF attachment (up to 1 MB).
5. Say whether you are below the poverty line. If yes, upload your BPL certificate and submit without paying.
6. Otherwise pay ₹10 by net banking, debit or credit card, RuPay or UPI.
7. Save the **registration number** shown on screen and emailed to you. You need it to track status, pay any extra fee and appeal.

If money is debited but no number appears, use **Payment Reconciliation** rather than paying again; the portal's FAQ says this can take 24 to 48 working hours.

Under the RTI Rules, 2012, copies cost ₹2 per page (A4 or A3), inspection is free for the first hour and ₹5 for each hour after, and postage is charged only above ₹50.

**State bodies** follow their own state RTI rules, fees and payment methods. In *Pravasi Legal Cell v Union of India* (March 2023), the Supreme Court directed all states and Union Territories to set up online RTI portals, and high courts to do the same for themselves and district courts. Where there is no portal, post the application to the PIO with the fee your state's rules set.

## Time limits at a glance

| Step | Deadline | Law |
|---|---|---|
| PIO's reply | 30 days from receipt | Section 7(1) |
| Information about a person's life or liberty | 48 hours | Section 7(1) |
| Application given to an Assistant PIO | 5 extra days | Section 5(2) |
| Transfer to the right office, if sent to the wrong one | Within 5 days | Section 6(3) |
| Your first appeal | 30 days after the reply or the missed deadline | Section 19(1) |
| First appeal decided | 30 days, at most 45 | Section 19(6) |
| Your second appeal to the CIC or SIC | 90 days after the first appeal decision was due or received | Section 19(3) |

## What to do if you get no reply

Section 7(2) says no decision within the time limit counts as a **refusal**, so you don't have to keep waiting.

1. **First appeal.** On rtionline.gov.in, choose Submit First Appeal and enter your registration number and email. There is no fee. It goes to the **First Appellate Authority**, an officer senior to the PIO in the same office. The portal's FAQ notes that online first appeals work only for requests filed online.
2. **Second appeal.** If the first appeal is not answered or doesn't help, go to the **Central Information Commission** (central bodies) or your **State Information Commission**. For central bodies you can file the second appeal online on the CIC's portal, dss.cic.gov.in. The Commission can order disclosure, compensation and penalties.
3. **Complaint.** Section 18 lets you complain straight to the Commission in cases such as no PIO being appointed or an unreasonable fee being demanded.

## What can be refused: Section 8

Section 8(1) lists ten grounds: national security and foreign relations; matters a court has forbidden; breach of legislative privilege; trade secrets; information held in trust (fiduciary); information from foreign governments; anything endangering a person's life or revealing a source; information that would impede an investigation; Cabinet papers until the decision is taken and the matter is complete; and personal information (clause j).

Two safeguards remain. Section 8(2) allows disclosure of exempt information when the public interest outweighs the harm, and Section 8(3) says most information more than 20 years old must be given.

According to the CIC's annual report for 2024-25, as reported by PTI in February 2026, central public authorities cited Section 8(1) 28,924 times, about half of all refusal grounds recorded.

## Two changes to know about

### 2019: commissioners' tenure and pay

The RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019 ended information commissioners' fixed five-year term and their pay parity with election commissioners, letting the Centre set both by rules. Rules notified in October 2019 fixed the term at three years and monthly pay at ₹2.5 lakh for the Chief Information Commissioner and ₹2.25 lakh for Information Commissioners. The government said the Election Commission is a constitutional body and the Information Commission a statutory one, so equating them was an anomaly. Critics, including former Central Information Commissioners, said government control over tenure and pay would weaken commissioners' independence.

### 2023: the personal information exemption

Section 44(3) of the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 rewrote Section 8(1)(j). The old clause let officers refuse personal information only if it had no link to public activity or would unwarrantedly invade privacy, and allowed disclosure where the larger public interest justified it. The new clause simply exempts information "which relates to personal information". It came into force in November 2025, when the government notified the DPDP Rules, 2025.

The government's explanatory note, published through PIB in November 2025, says the change balances transparency with the right to privacy recognised in the Supreme Court's *Puttaswamy* judgment, and that Section 8(2)'s public-interest test still applies. Critics, including RTI activists and digital-rights groups such as SFLC.in, say it removes the officer's duty to weigh the larger public interest under clause (j), which could shield details about public officials and make reporting on wrongdoing harder. Petitions challenging it are before the Supreme Court, which in February 2026 referred them to a larger bench and declined to stay the law; the case is still pending.

## How busy is the system?

The CIC's 2024-25 report, as reported by PTI, shows the Ministry of Corporate Affairs received 2,54,657 RTI applications and the Ministry of Finance 2,20,283. The government told the Rajya Sabha in February and March 2026 that the rejection rate at central public authorities fell from 7.21% in 2013-14 to 3.26% in 2024-25, and that 29,034 second appeals and 3,577 complaints were pending before the CIC on 1 February 2026.

RTI is often the quickest way to move a stuck file, such as a pension halted after a missed [life certificate](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-submit-life-certificate-for-pension). ## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file an RTI application without giving a reason?

Yes. Section 6(2) says you need not give any reason or any personal details beyond those needed to contact you.

### What happens if the PIO does not reply in 30 days?

The request is treated as refused under Section 7(2). You can file a free first appeal within 30 days, and any information supplied late must be given free.

### Can I use rtionline.gov.in for state government offices?

No. The central portal covers only central government bodies, and applications for state bodies are returned without a refund. Use your state's own portal or apply by post.

### Is there a fee for the first appeal?

No. For central public authorities the first appeal is free, online or on paper. Only the original application carries the ₹10 fee (copy charges apart).
