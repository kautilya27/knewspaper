**Quick answer:** Eight speeches that still teach anyone who has to stand up and talk are Swami Vivekananda at Chicago (1893), Mahatma Gandhi's "Do or Die" (1942), Jawaharlal Nehru's "Tryst with Destiny" (1947), Dr B.R. Ambedkar's closing speech to the Constituent Assembly (1949), Abraham Lincoln at Gettysburg (1863), Winston Churchill's "We shall fight on the beaches" (1940), Martin Luther King Jr's "I Have a Dream" (1963) and Nelson Mandela's statement from the dock (1964).

Most online lists of great speeches are almost entirely American and British, and rarely say *why* a speech worked. Here each one gets its exact date and place, one short line checked against a reliable transcript, the technique that made it land, and where to read the original. Dr Ambedkar's speech was delivered on 25 November 1949, the day before the Constitution was adopted, and it is widely recalled on Constitution Day.

## The eight speeches at a glance

| Speech | Date and place | The technique |
|---|---|---|
| Vivekananda, Response to Welcome | 11 Sept 1893, World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago | An opening that turns strangers into family |
| Gandhi, Quit India | 8 Aug 1942, Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay | A whole message in two words |
| Nehru, Tryst with Destiny | 11 pm, 14 Aug 1947, Constitution Hall, New Delhi | The moment itself as the image |
| Ambedkar, closing speech | 25 Nov 1949, Constituent Assembly, New Delhi | A hard truth on a day of celebration |

## Great Indian speeches

### Swami Vivekananda, Chicago, 11 September 1893

At the first World's Parliament of Religions, on the site where the Art Institute of Chicago now stands, the young monk opened with "Sisters and Brothers of America". According to the text published by the Ramakrishna Math at Belur, he went on: "I am proud to belong to a religion which has taught the world both tolerance and universal acceptance."

**Why it worked:** he treated a foreign audience as family before making his case for tolerance. The full text is on the [Belur Math website](https://belurmath.org/swami-vivekananda-speeches-at-the-parliament-of-religions-chicago-1893/).

### Mahatma Gandhi, Quit India, 8 August 1942

Gandhi spoke at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay as the Congress passed the Quit India resolution. The Press Information Bureau's Quit India archive quotes him: "Here is a mantra, a short one that I give you... The mantra is Do or Die." He explained: "We shall either free India or die in the attempt." According to the PIB, the major leaders were arrested within 24 hours, so the message had to travel without them.

**Why it worked:** a long, careful speech ended in a slogan short enough to be shouted and passed on by people who never heard it. Read it in the *Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi* (Publications Division, Government of India) or the PIB's Quit India archive.

### Jawaharlal Nehru, Tryst with Destiny, 14–15 August 1947

According to the official Constituent Assembly Debates, the Assembly met at 11 pm on 14 August 1947 in the Constitution Hall, New Delhi. Nehru said: "Long years ago we made a tryst with destiny, and now the time comes when we shall redeem our pledge, not wholly or in full measure, but very substantially."

**Why it worked:** he used the hour itself as his picture ("At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom"). And he was honest in the same breath: "not wholly or in full measure". On a night that also brought the [Partition of India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/partition-of-india-and-pakistan), admitting what was unfinished made the rest believable. The text is in the Parliament digital library (eparlib.sansad.in).

### Dr B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly, 25 November 1949

The day before the Constitution was adopted (26 November, now Constitution Day), the chairman of the Drafting Committee did not simply celebrate. He said: "On the 26th of January 1950, we are going to enter into a life of contradictions. In politics we will have equality and in social and economic life we will have inequality."

He also warned that "in politics, Bhakti or hero-worship is a sure road to degradation and to eventual dictatorship", and called unconstitutional methods of protest, once a constitution existed, "the Grammar of Anarchy".

**Why it worked:** a contrast anyone can repeat, placed in the moment of success, when people were most ready to listen. Read the [Constituent Assembly Debates for 25 November 1949](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/763285/1/cad_25-11-1949.pdf); the Supreme Court of India also hosts the speech on its website.

## Great world speeches

### Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg, 19 November 1863

Dedicating a Civil War military cemetery, Lincoln spoke roughly 270 words, according to the Library of Congress, which holds two copies in his handwriting. He ended by asking that "government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth."

**Why it worked:** short enough to remember whole, with a three-part closing phrase (a "tricolon") that the ear hears as complete.

### Winston Churchill, House of Commons, 4 June 1940

After the evacuation from Dunkirk, Churchill spoke in the Commons from about 3.40 pm to 4.14 pm, according to historian Richard Toye on the UK government's history blog. He promised "we shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds", building to "we shall never surrender". A detail often missed: he did not broadcast it that evening. A BBC announcer read parts on the news, and Churchill recorded it only in 1949.

**Why it worked:** the same three words again and again, almost entirely in short, plain words. Repetition gives listeners a rhythm to hold when they can't see the text.

### Martin Luther King Jr, Washington DC, 28 August 1963

At the end of the March on Washington, before a crowd Britannica puts at about 250,000, King repeated "I have a dream" again and again. The best-known line hopes his children "will not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character." According to Britannica, he left his prepared text after the singer Mahalia Jackson urged him to tell the crowd about "the dream".

**Why it worked:** a refrain at the start of each sentence (called anaphora), and a speaker who knew his theme well enough to drop his notes.

### Nelson Mandela, Pretoria, 20 April 1964

Facing the Rivonia Trial, Mandela chose to make a statement from the dock instead of giving evidence. The Nelson Mandela Foundation says he spoke for over four hours, ending: "It is an ideal which I hope to live for and to achieve. But if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die." Less than two months later he was sentenced to life imprisonment.

**Why it worked:** hours of calm, step-by-step reasoning, then a last line that put his own life on the table. The foundation and the United Nations both publish the statement.

## What students and speakers can borrow

Each tip comes from a speech above.

1. **Greet like Vivekananda.** Instead of "Respected principal, teachers and my dear friends", open with a line that tells the audience how you see them.
2. **End with a Gandhi-style mantra.** Before writing anything, choose the four or five words you want people to repeat on the way out.
3. **Add Nehru's honest clause.** Admitting what isn't perfect makes your main claim more believable, in a speech or when you [answer "Tell me about yourself"](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/tell-me-about-yourself).
4. **Build one contrast, as Ambedkar did.** "Equality in politics, inequality in society" sticks better than a paragraph of explanation.
5. **Cut to Lincoln's length.** About 270 words takes roughly two minutes to say.
6. **Repeat like Churchill and King.** Return to one phrase three or more times. It looks odd on paper; out loud it guides the listener.
7. **Know it well enough to drop the notes.** King could leave his script because he had used the "dream" theme before. Practise until you speak from points, not sentences; it also helps in [building self-confidence](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-to-build-self-confidence).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most famous speech in Indian history?

There is no official ranking, but Nehru's "Tryst with Destiny" of 14–15 August 1947 is among the most quoted. Vivekananda's Chicago address (1893) and Gandhi's "Do or Die" speech (1942) are the other usual answers.

### When did Dr Ambedkar give his famous Constituent Assembly speech?

On 25 November 1949, the day before the Assembly adopted the Constitution. The full text is in the Constituent Assembly Debates on the Parliament digital library.

### Where did Gandhi give the Quit India speech?

At Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay (now Mumbai) on 8 August 1942, where he gave the mantra "Do or Die". Leaders were arrested within a day, according to the PIB's Quit India archive.

### Which famous speech is known for being short?

Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, at roughly 270 words according to the Library of Congress, is the classic example.
