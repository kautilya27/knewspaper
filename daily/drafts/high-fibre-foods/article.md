**Quick answer:** The richest everyday sources of fibre in an Indian kitchen are whole pulses (kala chana, rajma, whole moong, soybean), whole grains and millets (atta, bajra, ragi, jowar), and fruits and vegetables such as guava, amla, methi, bhindi and green peas. Linseed (alsi) is the most concentrated of all. According to the Indian Food Composition Tables 2017 from ICMR's National Institute of Nutrition (NIN), whole Bengal gram has about 25 g of fibre per 100 g, while milled white rice has under 3 g.

How much do you need? The WHO advises adults to eat at least 25 g of naturally occurring fibre a day, and the NHS in the UK suggests 30 g. ICMR-NIN's 2020 nutrient requirements for Indians treat about 40 g per 2,000 kcal as a safe intake, but its Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) don't ask you to count grams. Instead they ask you to make at least half your cereals whole grains, eat pulses every day and have about 400 g of vegetables and 100 g of fruit. Follow that plate and fibre takes care of itself.

### Three things the table shows

- **Whole beats split.** Sabut moong has about 17 g of fibre per 100 g; the split, husked moong dal has about 9 g. Whole kala chana has 25 g; chana dal has 15 g. Removing the husk takes away a lot of fibre, so using whole pulses, sprouts or chilka dal more often is an easy win.
- **Millets and atta are on a par.** Bajra, ragi, jowar and whole wheat atta all sit around 10–11.5 g. White rice has about a quarter of that. You don't have to give up rice: swapping some of it for a millet roti, or mixing in brown rice, already helps.
- **Guava and amla are fibre fruits.** Gram for gram, a guava has more than three times the fibre of an apple and more than four times that of a ripe banana.

## Soluble and insoluble fibre: what's the difference?

Most plant foods contain both kinds; the table shows that insoluble fibre is the larger share in almost all of them.

- **Soluble fibre** dissolves in water and forms a gel in the gut. According to Cleveland Clinic, it can help lower cholesterol and blood sugar and feeds helpful gut bacteria. ICMR-NIN says fibre slows the absorption of carbohydrates and fats and helps you feel full for longer. In the IFCT table, soybean (5.0 g) and linseed (4.3 g) have the most soluble fibre, followed by pulses such as rajma and whole chana (about 2.5–2.7 g).
- **Insoluble fibre** doesn't dissolve. It adds bulk and holds water in stool, keeping you regular. Whole pulses, whole grains and millets are rich in it.

ICMR-NIN's guidelines link a fibre-rich diet to less constipation, a lower risk of piles, and a protective role in heart disease, diabetes and obesity. The NHS says eating plenty of fibre is linked with a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer. If you have diabetes, our guide to [diabetes diagnosis and treatment](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/diabetes-diagnosis-and-treatment) explains why your doctor will look at your whole diet, not one food.

## A sample Indian day with about 36 g of fibre

Here is one ordinary vegetarian day, worked out from the IFCT 2017 figures above (raw weights). It is an illustration of how the numbers add up, not a diet plan; portions depend on your age, activity and health.

| Meal | Food (raw weight) | Fibre (g) |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast | Whole moong chilla or sprouts, 40 g dry moong | 6.8 |
| Lunch | Rotis from 60 g atta | 6.8 |
| Lunch | Toor dal, 30 g dry | 2.7 |
| Lunch | Bhindi sabzi, 100 g | 4.1 |
| Evening | One guava, about 100 g | 8.6 |
| Dinner | White rice, 60 g raw | 1.7 |
| Dinner | Rajma, 30 g dry | 5.0 |
| **Total** | | **about 36** |

That is above the WHO's minimum of 25 g and the NHS's 30 g, without any special products. Onions, tomatoes, salad and a spoon of chutney would add a little more. Milk, curd and ghee add nothing to the fibre count.

For comparison, ICMR-NIN's own "My Plate for the Day" for a 2,000 kcal diet includes 250 g of cereals and millets, 85 g of pulses, 400 g of vegetables, 100 g of fruit and 35 g of nuts and seeds. Working this out with the average fibre per food group that ICMR-NIN publishes, the plate supplies roughly 40 g of fibre or more, depending on which grains and pulses you pick (our estimate, not a figure ICMR-NIN states). The catch is that most urban Indian adults eat far fewer vegetables and fruits than this; NIN's 2023 What India Eats report, using 2016 survey data, found urban adults averaging about 94 g of vegetables plus 89 g of roots and tubers a day, against the 400 g advised.

## How to add fibre without stomach trouble

- **Go slowly.** NHS hospital dietitians advise increasing fibre gradually, changing one thing at a time. A sudden jump can cause gas, bloating and stomach upset.
- **Drink enough water.** Fibre needs fluid to work. ICMR-NIN advises healthy adults to have about eight glasses (around 2 litres) of water and other drinks a day.
- **Eat whole fruit, not juice.** ICMR-NIN points out that juicing removes much of the fibre-rich pulp.
- **Watch processed foods.** According to ICMR-NIN, refined flours and ultra-processed snacks are depleted of fibre. Fibre also isn't a compulsory item on Indian food labels, so a packet may not tell you.
- **Food first.** Fibre powders and husk supplements are not a substitute for a varied diet. If you think you need one, ask your doctor.

## Who should check with a doctor first

More fibre is right for most people, but not for everyone, and not always all at once.

- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):** According to the NHS, people whose IBS causes diarrhoea may be advised to cut down on wholegrains, nuts and seeds, while those with constipation may do better with more soluble fibre. Get advice from your doctor or a dietitian.
- **Narrowing of the bowel (stricture), for example in Crohn's disease:** NHS hospital dietitians advise softer, lower-fibre foods for many people with a stricture, because high-fibre foods are harder to digest.
- **Chronic kidney disease:** some people are told to limit potassium or phosphate, and pulses and nuts are also counted as protein foods. Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Trust advises checking with your health professional before changing your diet.
- **After bowel surgery, or if you've been told to follow a low-fibre or low-residue diet:** follow your own doctor's advice.

### Bowel symptoms that need a doctor

See a doctor rather than just changing your diet if you notice any of these, which the NHS lists as possible signs of bowel cancer and other bowel problems: blood in your stool (red or black), bleeding from the bottom, a change in your usual bowel habit, often feeling you need to go even after you've been, tummy pain or a lump, or weight loss without trying. Black or dark red stools or bloody diarrhoea need an urgent appointment; heavy, non-stop bleeding needs emergency care.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Indian food has the highest fibre?

Among everyday foods in the IFCT 2017, linseeds (about 26 g per 100 g) and whole kala chana (about 25 g) are at the top, followed by soybean, whole moong and rajma. Among fruits, guava and amla lead.

### How much fibre should I eat in a day?

The WHO recommends at least 25 g a day for adults, and the NHS suggests 30 g. ICMR-NIN's 2020 nutrient requirements put a safe intake at about 40 g per 2,000 kcal; its 2024 guidelines focus on whole grains, daily pulses, and plenty of vegetables and fruit rather than a gram count.

### Does cooking dal destroy its fibre?

Boiling or pressure-cooking doesn't remove fibre, but cooked dal weighs more because it absorbs water, so fibre per 100 g of cooked dal looks lower. Calculate from the dry weight. Removing the husk does lower fibre, which is why whole pulses have more than split dals.

### Is rice bad because it is low in fibre?

No. White rice is low in fibre (about 2.8 g per 100 g raw), but it can sit in a high-fibre meal with dal, rajma and vegetables. ICMR-NIN suggests at least half your cereals be whole grains or millets.
