**Quick answer:** In India, a bill becomes a law when both Houses of Parliament pass it in the same form and the President gives assent. In each House it is introduced (first reading), debated and sometimes sent to a committee (second reading), and voted on (third reading). Once the President signs, it is an Act.

The route depends on the kind of bill. For a Money Bill, the Lok Sabha has the final say. A Constitution Amendment Bill needs a special majority in each House and sometimes the approval of half the states. A state bill goes to the Governor, and the Supreme Court's five-judge opinion of 20 November 2025 now sets out what the Governor can and cannot do.

## The six steps for an ordinary bill

The rules come from Articles 107 to 111 of the Constitution and the Rules of Procedure of each House. Most bills are Government Bills moved by a minister. A bill moved by any other MP is a Private Member's Bill.

| Step | What happens | Can the bill stop here? |
|---|---|---|
| 1. First reading | Leave to introduce; bill published in the Gazette | Yes, if leave is refused |
| 2. Second reading | Debate on principles; possible committee; clause-by-clause vote | Yes, if defeated or left pending |
| 3. Third reading | Vote on the bill as a whole | Yes, if defeated |
| 4. Other House | Same three readings | Deadlock can go to a joint sitting |
| 5. President | Assent, withhold, or return once | Only if assent is withheld |
| 6. Act | Comes into force on the date it names or the government notifies | n/a |

### First reading

The member asks the House for leave to introduce the bill. The Lok Sabha's own guide to Parliament says that if the motion is opposed, it is put to a vote after short statements from both sides. The content is not debated at this stage. An ordinary bill can start in either House.

### Second reading and committees

This is where the real work happens. The House first discusses the bill's principles. It can then take the bill up at once, send it to a Select Committee (members of one House) or a Joint Committee (both Houses), or circulate it for public opinion. The Speaker or the Rajya Sabha Chairman can also send it to a departmentally related standing committee. Committees go through the bill clause by clause, can hear experts and affected groups, and suggest changes. Their reports are advisory. The House then votes clause by clause on the bill and on any amendments.

Committee referral is at the presiding officer's discretion, and short or urgent bills often skip it. It has become less common. According to [PRS Legislative Research](https://prsindia.org/parliamenttrack/vital-stats/functioning-of-the-17th-lok-sabha), only 16% of bills in the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–2024) went to committees, fewer than in the three Lok Sabhas before it. PRS also found that 35% of bills passed in the Lok Sabha were discussed for less than an hour. PRS and other analysts argue that committees are where detailed, cross-party scrutiny happens.

### Third reading

The minister moves that the bill be passed. Only formal or consequential changes are allowed now. If a majority of members present and voting agree, the bill goes to the other House. That House runs the same three readings and can pass the bill, amend it (it then goes back to the first House), reject it, or not act on it.

### Joint sitting (Article 108)

If the other House rejects the bill, the Houses disagree on amendments, or six months pass without a decision, the President can call a joint sitting. The Speaker presides, and a majority of members of both Houses present and voting decides the bill. Because the Lok Sabha has more than twice as many members, it usually prevails. Joint sittings have been held only three times: on the Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978) and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill (2002). Lok Sabha records from March 2002 show the last one followed the Rajya Sabha rejecting a bill the Lok Sabha had passed.

### The President (Article 111)

The President, acting on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers, can give assent, withhold it, or return a bill other than a Money Bill for reconsideration. If Parliament passes a returned bill again, with or without changes, the President cannot withhold assent. Article 111 sets no deadline.

## A real example: the Income-tax Act, 2025

According to PRS, the Income-tax Bill, 2025 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 13 February 2025 and sent to a Select Committee, which tabled its report on 21 July 2025. Rather than move a long list of amendments, the government withdrew the bill on 8 August. On 11 August 2025 it introduced a revised Income-tax (No. 2) Bill which, Business Standard reported, took in most of the committee's recommendations. It was moved as a Money Bill, so the Rajya Sabha could only make recommendations. The Lok Sabha passed it that day and the Rajya Sabha returned it on 12 August. It had received assent by 21 August 2025 and applies from 1 April 2026. See the [old-to-new section mapping](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/new-income-tax-act-section-mapping) for how much was reorganised.

## Ordinary vs Money vs Constitution Amendment bills

| | Ordinary bill | Money Bill (Arts 109–110) | Constitution Amendment Bill (Art 368) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where it starts | Either House | Lok Sabha only, on the President's recommendation | Either House |
| Rajya Sabha's role | Equal: can amend or reject | Recommendations only, within 14 days; the Lok Sabha may reject them | Equal; must pass it separately |
| Majority needed | Members present and voting | Members present and voting | Majority of total membership plus two-thirds of those present and voting, in each House |
| Joint sitting? | Possible | No | No |
| States' approval | No | No | Half the state legislatures, for federal provisions such as the Seventh Schedule or High Courts |
| President | Assent, withhold or return once | Assent or withhold; cannot return | Must assent (24th Amendment, 1971) |

A Money Bill covers only the matters listed in Article 110, such as taxes, government borrowing and the Consolidated Fund of India. The Speaker certifies a bill as a Money Bill, and Article 110 makes that decision final. If the Rajya Sabha does not return a Money Bill within 14 days, it is treated as passed. Whether courts can review the Speaker's certificate has been disputed, and the Supreme Court has referred that question to a larger bench.

Amendments also have a limit. Since the Kesavananda Bharati judgment (1973), the Supreme Court has held that Parliament cannot change the Constitution's basic structure. That includes judicial review, federalism and the core of the [fundamental rights](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/fundamental-rights-vs-directive-principles).

## State bills and the Governor (Article 200)

A state bill goes through similar readings in the state legislature and then to the Governor. The Governor can give assent, withhold assent or reserve the bill for the President. A bill other than a Money Bill can be returned "as soon as possible" for the legislature to reconsider. If it is passed again, the Governor "shall not withhold assent". A bill that would weaken a High Court's powers must be reserved for the President.

Article 200 sets no time limit, and some bills have waited months or years. Three Supreme Court decisions followed:

- **State of Punjab v Principal Secretary to the Governor (10 November 2023):** A three-judge bench held that a Governor who withholds assent must send the bill back to the legislature. As reported by LiveLaw, the Court said otherwise the Governor could "thwart the normal course of lawmaking".
- **State of Tamil Nadu v Governor of Tamil Nadu (8 April 2025):** A two-judge bench gave the Governor one to three months to act and the President three months for reserved bills. Using Article 142, it treated ten pending Tamil Nadu bills as having received assent.
- **Presidential Reference under Article 143 (opinion of 20 November 2025):** In May 2025 the President referred 14 questions to the Supreme Court. A unanimous five-judge bench held that courts cannot set timelines for the Governor or the President and that there is no "deemed assent", including through Article 142. It held that the Governor has three options and cannot withhold assent without returning the bill, and that the merits of these decisions cannot be reviewed by courts. Where inaction is "prolonged, unexplained and indefinite", a court can direct the Governor to decide within a reasonable time without saying what the decision should be.

This opinion is the current position. It did not formally set aside the April judgment, but it rejected that judgment's timelines and deemed assent. States opposing the reference argued that without deadlines, bills could be stalled indefinitely. The bench's answer was that the Constitution deliberately left the time open. The full opinion is on the [Supreme Court website](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/39157/39157_2025_1_1501_66169_Judgement_20-Nov-2025.pdf).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take for a bill to become a law in India?

There is no fixed time. Some bills clear both Houses in two days, while others spend months with a committee. According to PRS, 58% of bills in the 17th Lok Sabha passed within two weeks of introduction.

### What happens to a bill when the Lok Sabha is dissolved?

A bill pending in the Lok Sabha, or passed by it and pending in the Rajya Sabha, lapses. A bill pending in the Rajya Sabha that the Lok Sabha has not passed does not lapse, and neither does a bill already sent to the President.

### Can the President reject a bill passed by Parliament?

The President can withhold assent, or return a non-Money Bill once for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again, the President must give assent. For Constitution Amendment Bills, assent is compulsory.

### Can a Governor keep a state bill pending forever?

No. According to the Supreme Court's November 2025 opinion, the Governor must choose one of three options and cannot withhold assent without returning the bill. Courts can order a decision within a reasonable time if the delay is prolonged and unexplained, but they cannot fix a deadline or treat the bill as passed.
