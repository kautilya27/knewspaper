**Quick answer:** Fiscal deficit is the gap between what the Union government spends in a year and what it earns without borrowing. That gap is filled by borrowing, so the fiscal deficit is, in effect, how much new debt the government takes on that year.

For 2026-27 the Union Budget puts it at ₹16,95,768 crore, or 4.3% of GDP. That is down from 4.4% in 2025-26, according to the Ministry of Finance's Budget at a Glance. The Budget watches the number closely because every rupee of deficit adds to public debt, and the interest on that debt becomes a fixed cost in every future Budget.

## Fiscal, revenue and primary deficit: the three numbers in every Budget

The Budget reports three deficits, and a toy example explains them faster than definitions.

Imagine a government whose year looks like this (₹ lakh crore):

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue receipts (taxes, dividends, fees) | 100 |
| Non-debt capital receipts (loan recoveries, disinvestment) | 5 |
| Revenue expenditure (salaries, subsidies, pensions, interest) | 110 |
| of which interest payments | 15 |
| Capital expenditure (roads, railways, loans to states) | 25 |

- **Fiscal deficit** = total spending minus receipts that are not borrowing = (110 + 25) − (100 + 5) = **30**. This is the government's borrowing need.
- **Revenue deficit** = revenue spending minus revenue receipts = 110 − 100 = **10**. It shows borrowing used to pay for day-to-day running costs rather than building assets.
- **Primary deficit** = fiscal deficit minus interest = 30 − 15 = **15**. It shows how much the government would still have to borrow if it had no old debt to service.

The Budget's own explanatory documents use the same definitions. Economists read the three together. A high fiscal deficit that mostly pays for capital spending is judged differently from one that pays for routine expenses.

## The real numbers: Union Budget 2026-27

These figures come from the Budget at a Glance presented on 1 February 2026. All amounts are in ₹ crore, with % of GDP in brackets.

| Indicator | 2024-25 Actuals | 2025-26 Revised | 2026-27 Budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal deficit | 15,74,431 (4.8%) | 15,58,492 (4.4%) | 16,95,768 (4.3%) |
| Revenue deficit | 5,64,296 (1.7%) | 5,26,764 (1.5%) | 5,92,344 (1.5%) |
| Primary deficit | 4,58,856 (1.4%) | 2,84,154 (0.8%) | 2,91,796 (0.7%) |
| Capital expenditure | 10,51,953 | 10,95,755 | 12,21,821 |

Two things stand out. First, the deficit grows in rupees but shrinks as a share of the economy. The Budget assumes 2026-27 GDP of about ₹393 lakh crore, roughly 10% more than the previous year, so the ratio matters more than the raw number (our explainer on [how India's GDP is calculated](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/gdp-india) covers that denominator).

Second, look at the gap between the fiscal and primary deficit. Subtracting one from the other gives interest payments of about ₹14 lakh crore in 2026-27. That is most of the year's borrowing, spent on servicing debt already taken.

For 2025-26, the Controller General of Accounts' provisional accounts showed the government met its 4.4% target, according to reports of the year-end data.

## How the government actually borrows

The Budget at a Glance also shows how the 2026-27 deficit is to be financed:

| Source | 2026-27 Budget (₹ crore) |
|---|---|
| Net market borrowings (dated G-secs) | 11,73,210 |
| Securities against small savings | 3,86,772 |
| Short-term borrowings (Treasury Bills etc.) | 1,30,000 |
| External debt | 15,385 |
| State Provident Funds | 3,500 |
| Drawing down cash balance | 32,702 |
| Other receipts (net) | −45,801 |
| **Total = fiscal deficit** | **16,95,768** |

- **G-secs (government securities)** are bonds that run from a few years to several decades. The RBI auctions them on the government's behalf to banks, insurers, provident funds, mutual funds and other investors.
- **Small savings** means your PPF, NSC, Sukanya Samriddhi or post office deposit. According to the Indian Economic Service's Arthapedia, all small savings deposits go into the National Small Savings Fund (NSSF), set up in 1999. The NSSF lends the net collections to the Centre and states through special 25-year securities. Some of your PPF money is funding the deficit.
- **Treasury Bills** are short-term IOUs of up to a year, used mainly to manage cash through the year.

## The FRBM Act and the switch to a debt target

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 is the law Parliament passed to bind governments to fiscal discipline. Like any Act, its targets can only be changed by an amendment ([here is how a Bill becomes law](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-a-bill-becomes-law-in-india)).

According to Business Standard, the 2018 amendment aimed to cut the fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP by 2020-21 and central debt to 40% of GDP by 2024-25. The pandemic overturned that path. The deficit rose from 4.6% of GDP in 2019-20 to about 9.2% in 2020-21, and the government has been bringing it down since.

In the 2025-26 Budget, the government changed its main target. From 2026-27, it aims to keep central government debt falling as a share of GDP, rather than hit a fixed deficit number each year. The stated goal is debt of "about 50±1 per cent" of GDP by 31 March 2031, down from 57.1% in 2024-25 (Business Standard, citing the Budget's fiscal policy statement). The 2026-27 Budget puts debt at 55.6% of GDP, down from 56.1% in 2025-26. Each year's deficit target is now worked out from that debt path.

The government says a debt target gives it more room to manoeuvre and is a better measure because it captures the effect of past decisions too.

## Where 2026-27 stands now

The latest monthly accounts from the Controller General of Accounts cover April–September 2026. They show a fiscal deficit of ₹7,14,413 crore, or 42.1% of the full-year target, compared with 36.5% at the same point last year (PTI, 30 October 2026). Capital spending was up about 13% year on year at ₹6.55 lakh crore, according to Business Standard. Subsidies, especially fertiliser, ran ahead of budget.

The April–October figures are due at the end of November. Opinion on the full-year target is divided. ICRA's chief economist Aditi Nayar told Business Standard that the government needs to curtail its revenue deficit in the second half, which may prove challenging. CareEdge estimated in November that the deficit could overshoot by ₹70,000–80,000 crore, putting it at 4.3–4.5% of GDP, because of weaker excise and income tax receipts and higher fertiliser subsidies. It added that higher dividends, disinvestment and a ₹1 lakh crore stabilisation fund could help the government stay on target. The final number will be known only in May 2027.

## Why rating agencies and economists watch it

- **Interest burden:** the larger the debt, the more of each year's tax money goes to interest before anything is spent on schools or roads.
- **Interest rates:** heavy government borrowing competes with companies for the same savings and can push up borrowing costs.
- **Inflation risk:** a deficit financed in ways that add a lot of money to the system can feed price rises (see [how inflation is measured in India](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/how-is-inflation-measured-in-india)).
- **Credit ratings:** S&P raised India's sovereign rating to BBB in August 2025, its first upgrade in 18 years, crediting the government's "commitment to fiscal consolidation". It also said India's consolidation is slower than that of peers with similar ratings, and named "burdensome debt stock" as a weakness.

## States have their own deficit limits

The Sixteenth Finance Commission, whose recommendations cover 2026–31, recommended capping each state's fiscal deficit at 3% of its GSDP. It said 50-year interest-free central loans to states for capital projects should sit outside that cap. It also asked states to stop off-budget borrowing (loans taken through state companies that never appear in the Budget). The Union government has accepted the borrowing ceilings in principle and will examine the other recommendations separately, according to reports of the Commission's report tabled with the Budget. The Commission also recommended that the Centre bring its own deficit down to 3.5% of GDP by 2030-31 ([PRS India's summary](https://prsindia.org/policy/report-summaries/report-of-the-16th-finance-commission-for-2026-31) has the details).

## Is a lower deficit always better? The counter-views

- **The case for spending in bad times:** former chief statistician Pronab Sen has argued (Business Standard, 2020) that reviving growth should come before protecting the deficit target, and that a higher deficit is acceptable if it funds rural schemes and infrastructure. An April 2023 RBI Bulletin article cites studies finding that public capital spending has a much bigger and longer-lasting effect on growth than revenue spending, especially when the economy has spare capacity.
- **The case for faster consolidation:** economist D K Srivastava of EY India has argued that dropping a fixed deficit path for a debt target makes the effort towards fiscal consolidation "somewhat vague". He also noted that under the new path debt would stay well above the FRBM Act's 40% norm, and that keeping debt high for long means ever-higher interest payments. Columnists have also noted that the 2026-27 cut of 0.1 percentage point is the smallest since the pandemic, leaving more to do later.
- **Quality, not just size:** many economists look at what the deficit pays for. Capital expenditure rising to ₹12.2 lakh crore in 2026-27 is seen as healthier than borrowing for subsidies, but critics point to underspending on some schemes in 2025-26.

The government's position is that it has cut the deficit steadily while protecting capital spending and welfare.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is fiscal deficit the same as government debt?
No. Fiscal deficit is one year's borrowing. Debt is the total built up over all past years. Each year's deficit adds to the debt.

### What is India's fiscal deficit target for 2026-27?
The Union Budget 2026-27 targets ₹16,95,768 crore, or 4.3% of GDP, according to the Budget at a Glance.

### Who publishes the monthly fiscal deficit data?
The Controller General of Accounts (CGA) in the Ministry of Finance releases the Centre's monthly accounts, usually on the last working day of the following month.

### Why does the government borrow from small savings?
Money deposited in PPF, NSC and similar schemes goes into the National Small Savings Fund. The fund lends it to the Centre and states through special securities, which makes it a steady, long-term source of borrowing.
