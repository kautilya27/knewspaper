Portugal is one of Europe's best countries for a riding holiday that is kind to horses and to the landscape. It has a deep horse culture built around the **Lusitano**, quiet trails through cork forests and olive groves in the Alentejo, the wild mountain country of Peneda-Gerês in the north, and a growing number of centres that train horses gently and keep groups small.

**Quick answer:** for ethical horse riding in Portugal, choose a centre where the horses look healthy and relaxed, training is calm and reward-based, groups are small, rides are matched to your level and routes stay on marked trails. The Alentejo suits gentle multi-day trail rides, the Ribatejo is the heart of Lusitano breeding, the north offers mountain riding, and Lisbon is where you can watch classical Portuguese riding at its best.

## The Lusitano: Portugal's own horse

The Lusitano comes from the Iberian Peninsula and takes its name from Lusitania, the Roman name for the region that is now Portugal. For centuries Portuguese and Spanish horses of this type were simply called Iberian or Andalusian; Portugal adopted the name Lusitano in 1966, when the two countries' studbooks were separated.

Lusitanos are known for their agility, intelligence and willing, calm temperament. Historically they were bred as war horses and for mounted bullfighting, and they are now widely used in classical dressage and working equitation. Lusitanos have competed at the Olympics in Portuguese and Spanish dressage teams.

The breed is closely tied to Portuguese history. The royal stud at **Alter do Chão** in the Alentejo, the Coudelaria de Alter, was founded in 1748 to supply horses for the royal riding school, and its Alter Real line is still bred there today.

## Where to see classical Portuguese riding

The best place to see Lusitanos in their traditional role is the **Portuguese School of Equestrian Art** (Escola Portuguesa de Arte Equestre). According to Parques de Sintra, which manages it, the school is based in the gardens of the National Palace of Queluz, near Lisbon, and gives its regular public presentations at the Henrique Calado Riding Ring in Belém, Lisbon. Its horses are Lusitanos bred by the Coudelaria de Alter. Check the school's calendar on the Parques de Sintra website for dates and tickets before you go.

If you are in Portugal in November, the **Feira Nacional do Cavalo** (National Horse Fair) in Golegã, in the Ribatejo, is the country's biggest horse festival. It is held every year around St Martin's Day (11 November); event listings give the 2026 dates as 6 to 15 November.

## Best regions for a riding holiday

| Region | What the riding is like | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Alentejo | Rolling hills, cork oak and olive groves, quiet villages, long trails | Multi-day trail rides, relaxed riders |
| Ribatejo (Golegã area) | Flat river plains and horse-breeding farms | Lusitano stud visits, dressage lessons |
| Peneda-Gerês (north) | Mountains, granite villages, steep tracks | Experienced riders, nature lovers |
| Algarve and coast | Countryside, coastal paths and some beach rides | Short rides on a beach holiday |
| Lisbon and Sintra | Classical riding shows, lessons in the hills | Watching classical riding, short lessons |

**Peneda-Gerês** is Portugal's only national park, created in 1971. It is also home to the **Garrano**, a small native horse that lives semi-wild in the mountains in groups of one stallion with several mares. The breed is listed as at risk, so if you see Garranos while riding or hiking, keep your distance and never feed them.

## What makes horse riding ethical?

"Ethical" is not a legal label, so judge each centre by what you can see and ask. A good centre will be happy to answer.

**Signs of a well-run centre**

- Horses are a healthy weight, with clean coats, healthy hooves and no sores from tack.
- They have shelter, clean water, hay and time loose in a field or paddock with other horses, not only in stables.
- Horses get rest days, and the same horse is not used for ride after ride in the midday heat.
- Training is calm and reward-based, without hitting, harsh bits, yanking or tight draw reins.
- Staff ask about your weight, height and riding level and choose a horse to match.
- Groups are small, guides are experienced and you are given a helmet.
- Rides stay on marked trails and respect farmland, wildlife and protected areas.

**Warning signs**

- Thin, lame or listless horses, or horses that pin their ears and flinch when handled.
- Guides who push horses to gallop for photos or ignore a horse that is struggling.
- No questions about your experience, or no safety briefing.
- Very cheap rides with large groups, which often means horses are overworked.

If animal welfare is your main concern, you can also ask whether a centre's horses are used in bullfighting events, and choose one that does not.

## Sustainable riding: lower impact on the land

Riding is one of the gentlest ways to see rural Portugal: no engine noise, little erosion on well-kept trails and money spent in small villages. To keep it that way:

- Choose operators that keep group sizes small and use established trails, especially in protected areas such as Peneda-Gerês.
- Stay in locally run guesthouses or farm stays (often called *quintas* or *montes*) on multi-day trails.
- Follow "leave no trace": take all litter home, close farm gates and keep noise down near livestock and wildlife.
- Travel between regions by train or bus where you can, and ride in spring or autumn when the heat is easier on horses.

## Rescue horses and equine-assisted activities

Some centres and sanctuaries in Portugal take in neglected or retired horses, rehabilitate them and rehome them, and a few offer equine-assisted activities such as groundwork and mindfulness sessions with horses. These can be rewarding to visit or support. Before you book, check that the organisation is open about how many horses it keeps, how they are cared for and who runs any therapy sessions; equine-assisted therapy should be led by qualified professionals and is not a replacement for medical care.

## Planning a trip from India

- **Visa:** Portugal is in the Schengen area, so Indian passport holders need a Schengen short-stay (Type C) visa, which allows up to 90 days in any 180-day period. The standard fee for adults is €90, and you can apply up to six months before travel and no later than 15 days before. Apply to Portugal if it is your main destination.
- **Entry checks:** the EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) has been fully in use since April 2026, so your fingerprints and photo are recorded at the border on your first entry.
- **Insurance:** Schengen visas need travel medical insurance. Many policies exclude adventure activities, so check that horse riding is covered.
- **Riding level:** be honest about your experience when booking. Multi-day trails often expect you to be comfortable at walk, trot and canter for several hours a day; beginners are better off with lessons and short rides.
- **When to go:** spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the most comfortable seasons for both riders and horses. Summer in the Alentejo can be very hot.

## The bottom line

Portugal offers some of Europe's most rewarding riding, from classical dressage in Lisbon to quiet trails across the Alentejo. The most ethical choice is a centre that puts its horses' health first, rides in small groups on marked trails and is open about how it works. Pick carefully, ride within your level, and your trip will be good for you, the horse and the countryside. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Lusitano horse?

The Lusitano is a horse breed from the Iberian Peninsula and a symbol of Portugal's equestrian heritage. It is valued for its agility, intelligence and calm, willing temperament, and has traditionally been used as a war horse, in mounted bullfighting and in classical dressage.

### Where can you go horse riding in Portugal?

Popular areas include the rolling hills, olive groves and cork forests of the Alentejo, the mountain trails of Peneda-Gerês in the north, the Ribatejo with its strong horse-breeding tradition, and the countryside and coast of the Algarve. Each suits a different level of rider and type of trail.

### How can I tell if a horse riding centre is ethical?

Look for horses that are a healthy weight, calm and well rested, with shelter, water and time in a field. Ethical centres use gentle training without harsh force, match riders to suitable horses, keep groups small, stick to marked trails and are happy to answer questions about how their horses are cared for.

### Do I need riding experience for a horse riding holiday in Portugal?

Not always. Many riding centres offer lessons and gentle short rides for beginners, while multi-day trails and mountain terrain are better suited to experienced riders. Tell the operator your real level when booking so they can choose a suitable horse and route.
