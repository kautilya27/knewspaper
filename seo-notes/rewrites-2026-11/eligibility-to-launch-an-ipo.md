**Quick answer:** to launch a mainboard IPO in India, a company must meet SEBI's eligibility rules under the ICDR Regulations: either the **profitability route** (net tangible assets of at least ₹3 crore, an average operating profit of at least ₹15 crore with a profit in each of the last three years, and a net worth of at least ₹1 crore in each of those years) or the **QIB route** for companies that don't qualify, where at least **75% of the issue goes to qualified institutional buyers**. Small companies can instead list on the **SME platforms**, which since 2025 require an operating profit of at least ₹1 crore in two of the last three years.

Beyond these tests, the promoters and directors must have a clean regulatory record, all promoter shares must be in demat form, the promoters must lock in at least 20% of the company after listing, and the company must offer enough shares to the public. Here is each requirement explained, with the rules as they stand in 2026.

## What is an IPO?

An Initial Public Offering is when a company sells its shares to the public for the first time and lists them on a stock exchange such as the NSE or BSE. The money can come from **new shares** (a fresh issue, which raises money for the company) or from **existing shareholders selling** (an offer for sale, where the money goes to the sellers). Most Indian IPOs combine both.

## Mainboard IPO eligibility: the profitability route

Under Regulation 6(1) of the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018, a company can do a normal IPO if it meets **all** of these:

| Test | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Net tangible assets | At least ₹3 crore in each of the preceding three full years, with not more than 50% held as monetary assets (this cap doesn't apply if the IPO is entirely an offer for sale) |
| Profitability | Average operating profit of at least ₹15 crore over the preceding three years, with an operating profit in each of the three years |
| Net worth | At least ₹1 crore in each of the preceding three full years |
| Name change | If the company changed its name in the last year, at least 50% of the previous year's revenue must come from the activity suggested by the new name |

Companies using this route must reserve **at least 35% of the offer for retail investors** and at least 15% for non-institutional investors, with up to 50% for qualified institutional buyers (QIBs).

## The QIB route for loss-making companies

A company that fails any of the tests above, such as a fast-growing tech company that has never made a profit, can still list under Regulation 6(2). The condition is that **at least 75% of the net offer must be allotted to QIBs** such as mutual funds, banks, insurers and foreign portfolio investors. Retail investors get up to 10% and non-institutional investors up to 15%. If QIBs don't take up their 75%, the money is refunded. Several Indian new-age companies have listed this way.

## Conditions every IPO company must meet

- **Clean record:** the company, its promoters, promoter group, directors and selling shareholders must not be barred by SEBI from the securities market. Promoters and directors must not be wilful defaulters, fraudulent borrowers or fugitive economic offenders.
- **Demat shares:** all shares held by the promoters must be in dematerialised form before filing.
- **Fully paid-up shares:** existing partly paid shares must be fully paid up or forfeited.
- **No outstanding convertibles:** convertible securities that could turn into shares later must generally be converted before the IPO, with some exceptions such as employee stock options.
- **Stock exchange approval:** the company needs in-principle approval from the exchanges where it wants to list, each of which has its own listing criteria.

## Promoter contribution and lock-in

SEBI wants promoters to keep "skin in the game" after listing.

- **Minimum promoters' contribution:** promoters must hold at least **20% of the post-issue capital**, which is locked in.
- **Lock-in period:** that 20% is locked in for **18 months** from allotment, or **3 years** if the money raised is mainly for capital expenditure on a project. Promoter shares above the 20% are locked in for **6 months**, or 1 year in the capital-expenditure case.
- **Other pre-IPO shareholders:** shares held by non-promoter shareholders before the IPO are generally locked in for 6 months.
- **Anchor investors:** half of their shares are locked in for 30 days and the rest for 90 days.

These are shorter than the old 3-year and 1-year lock-ins that many older articles still quote; SEBI cut them in 2021.

## Minimum public offer vs minimum public shareholding

These two rules are often mixed up.

**Minimum public offer (at the IPO).** Under the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Rules, how much a company must offer in its IPO depends on its size. The rules were revised in March 2026 to make very large listings easier:

| Post-issue market capitalisation | Minimum offer to the public |
|---|---|
| Up to ₹1,600 crore | 25% |
| ₹1,600 crore to ₹4,000 crore | Shares worth ₹400 crore |
| ₹4,000 crore to ₹50,000 crore | 10% |
| ₹50,000 crore to ₹1 lakh crore | ₹1,000 crore and at least 8% |
| ₹1 lakh crore to ₹5 lakh crore | ₹6,250 crore and at least 2.75% |
| Above ₹5 lakh crore | ₹15,000 crore and at least 1% |

**Minimum public shareholding (after listing).** Every listed company must eventually have at least **25% of its shares held by the public**. Companies that list with less get time to reach it: three years for most, and longer (up to ten years) for the very largest.

## SME IPO eligibility and the 2025 changes

Smaller companies can list on **NSE Emerge** or **BSE SME**, where the rules are lighter but were tightened considerably in 2025 after concerns about inflated SME issues.

**Who can use the SME route:** companies with post-issue paid-up capital of up to ₹25 crore. Companies with up to ₹10 crore must use it. Each exchange adds its own conditions, such as a three-year operating track record and a positive net worth.

**Key changes SEBI made in 2025:**

- **Profit test:** an operating profit (EBITDA) of at least **₹1 crore in two of the three preceding financial years**.
- **Offer for sale capped:** an offer for sale can be at most **20% of the issue size**, and selling shareholders can sell no more than **50% of their holding**.
- **Use of money:** money for "general corporate purposes" is capped at **15% of the issue or ₹10 crore**, whichever is lower, and IPO money cannot be used to repay loans from promoters, the promoter group or related parties.
- **Staggered lock-in:** promoter shares above the minimum contribution are released in two halves, after one year and after two years.
- **Bigger applications:** individual investors must apply for at least two lots, which works out to more than ₹2 lakh, to keep very small retail investors out of risky issues.
- **More scrutiny:** the draft offer document is open for public comments for 21 days, and allotment to non-institutional investors is now by draw of lots, as on the mainboard.

SME IPOs must also be fully underwritten and have a market maker for at least three years after listing.

## Steps to launch an IPO in India

1. **Appoint advisers:** SEBI-registered merchant bankers (book running lead managers), legal counsel, auditors and a registrar.
2. **Prepare the company:** restate financial statements, strengthen the board and governance, and convert to a public limited company if needed.
3. **File the DRHP** with SEBI and the stock exchanges. Companies can also use SEBI's confidential pre-filing route.
4. **Public comments and SEBI observations:** the draft is published for comments, and SEBI issues its observations.
5. **File the red herring prospectus** with the Registrar of Companies, announce the price band and hold roadshows.
6. **Open the issue:** anchor investors bid a day before; the issue stays open for at least three working days.
7. **Allot and list:** shares are allotted and listed within **three working days** after the issue closes (the T+3 timeline).

Investors usually apply through UPI on their broker's app; our comparison of [Upstox, Groww and Zerodha](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/best-stock-exchange-app-upstox-or-grow-or-zerodha) covers the main platforms.

## Common reasons IPOs are delayed or rejected

- Weak or inconsistent financial statements.
- Pending litigation or regulatory action against the company or promoters.
- Vague plans for how the money will be used.
- Incomplete disclosures in the DRHP, which leads SEBI to return or hold back the document.

## Why the rules matter

SEBI's eligibility rules exist to protect investors from companies that are not ready to be public, a lesson India learned from scandals such as the [Harshad Mehta securities scam](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/who-was-harshad-mehta) that pushed SEBI to become a statutory regulator. For investors, an IPO is still a risky bet, so read the offer document, not just the grey market buzz. If you're weighing direct shares against funds, see [stocks vs mutual funds](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/stocks-vs-mutual-funds). The full regulations and latest amendments are on the [SEBI website](https://www.sebi.gov.in).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a loss-making company launch an IPO in India?

Yes. A company that does not meet SEBI's profitability and track-record tests can still go public through the alternative route under Regulation 6(2), as long as at least 75% of the issue is allotted to qualified institutional buyers such as mutual funds, banks and insurance companies. Several new-age tech companies have listed in India this way.

### What is the difference between a mainboard IPO and an SME IPO?

An SME IPO is meant for smaller companies, with post-issue paid-up capital of up to ₹25 crore, which list on separate platforms such as NSE Emerge and BSE SME with simpler requirements. Larger companies list on the main boards of BSE and NSE. Since 2025, SME IPOs need an operating profit of at least ₹1 crore in two of the last three years and face a cap on offers for sale.

### What is a DRHP in an IPO?

A Draft Red Herring Prospectus is the first offer document a company files with SEBI before an IPO. It discloses the company's financials, business risks, promoters, legal cases and how it plans to use the money raised, but not the final price or number of shares, which come later in the red herring prospectus.

### Can promoters sell their shares right after an IPO?

No. Promoters must keep at least 20% of the post-issue capital, and those shares are locked in for 18 months, or three years if the money is mainly for capital expenditure. Any extra promoter shares are locked in for six months, or one year in that case. SME IPOs have their own, longer lock-in rules.
