**Quick answer:** A mutual fund and an ETF (exchange-traded fund) both pool money to buy a basket of shares, bonds or gold. The difference is how you buy them. You buy **mutual fund** units from the fund house at the day's NAV, without a demat account, and can start a **SIP from as little as ₹100 to ₹500 a month**. You buy **ETF** units on the NSE or BSE like a share, at live prices, through a broker and a **demat account**. ETFs are usually passive and cheaper; mutual funds give you more choice, including actively managed funds and easy SIPs.

For most Indian beginners, the practical choice is between an **index fund** (a mutual fund that tracks an index) and an **ETF tracking the same index**. Both are taxed the same way in India, so the decision comes down to cost, convenience and whether you already have a demat account. The details below were checked on 5 November 2026.

## What are mutual funds and ETFs?

- **Mutual funds** collect money from many investors and invest it according to the scheme's stated goal, for example large-cap shares, government bonds or a mix. They can be **actively managed** (a fund manager picks investments to try to beat an index) or **passive** (index funds that copy an index such as the Nifty 50).
- **ETFs** also hold a basket of assets, but their units are listed and traded on the stock exchange. Most Indian ETFs are passive and track an index (Nifty 50, Sensex, Nifty Bank), gold, silver or government bonds.

Both are run by SEBI-registered asset management companies (AMCs) and regulated by **SEBI**. Neither guarantees returns; both carry market risk.

## Mutual funds vs ETFs at a glance

| Feature | Mutual funds | ETFs |
|---|---|---|
| Where you buy | From the AMC, a registrar, an app or a distributor | On NSE or BSE through a stockbroker |
| Demat account | Not needed | Needed |
| Price | NAV declared once a day | Live market price during trading hours |
| Minimum amount | SIPs often from ₹100 to ₹500; lump sums usually ₹100 to ₹5,000, depending on the fund | Price of one unit, often under ₹100 to a few hundred rupees |
| SIP | Built in and automatic | Not built in; some brokers offer a recurring buy |
| Management | Active or passive | Mostly passive |
| Costs | Expense ratio; exit load on some funds | Lower expense ratio, plus brokerage, STT and exchange charges when you trade |
| Liquidity | Redeem with the AMC at NAV | Depends on trading volume; thinly traded ETFs can be hard to sell at a fair price |
| Tax | Same rules by asset type | Same rules by asset type |

## Key differences explained

### 1. How you buy and sell

When you invest in a mutual fund, your order is processed at the NAV for that day, depending on when your money reaches the fund. You cannot see the exact price when you place the order, and you cannot trade during the day. SEBI requires redemption money to be paid within three working days, and equity funds often pay sooner.

An ETF trades on the exchange between 9:15 am and 3:30 pm. You can use market or limit orders and see the price before you buy. Because the price comes from buyers and sellers, it can sit slightly above or below the ETF's actual value. Check the **iNAV** (indicative NAV) shown by the exchange or the AMC before placing an order.

### 2. Demat account and KYC

You need a completed KYC for both. For ETFs you also need a **demat and trading account** with a broker. Mutual fund units can be held in a simple statement of account without demat, through the AMC's website, MF Central, registrar platforms or investment apps. If you are choosing a broker for ETFs, our comparison of [Zerodha, Groww and Upstox](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/best-stock-exchange-app-upstox-or-grow-or-zerodha) covers charges and features.

### 3. Costs and expense ratios

Every fund charges an annual **expense ratio**, taken out of the NAV. Under SEBI's new mutual fund regulations, in force from 1 April 2026, the cap is expressed as a **base expense ratio**, with GST, STT and other statutory levies charged separately at actual cost. For **index funds and ETFs**, the base cap is **0.9%** a year; in practice many large Nifty 50 ETFs and index funds charge far less. Actively managed funds can charge more, with caps that fall as the fund grows.

Always pick the **direct plan** of a mutual fund if you do not need a distributor. Direct plans skip distributor commission and have a lower expense ratio than regular plans of the same fund.

ETFs have one extra cost layer: you pay brokerage (many brokers charge nothing for delivery trades), STT, exchange charges, stamp duty and a DP charge when you sell. For a small monthly investment, these charges can cancel out the ETF's lower expense ratio.

### 4. Liquidity and tracking

An index fund always lets you redeem at NAV. An ETF is only as liquid as its trading volume. Popular Nifty 50, Sensex, gold and Bharat Bond ETFs trade heavily, but many smaller ETFs see little trading, so you may have to accept a worse price. Before buying, check daily volume and the gap between buy and sell prices.

Also compare **tracking error**, the gap between the fund's returns and the index it copies. Lower is better for both index funds and ETFs.

### 5. Transparency

Indian mutual funds must publish their full portfolio every month. Index funds and ETFs simply copy an index, so you always know roughly what you own, and AMCs publish ETF values through the day.

## How mutual funds and ETFs are taxed in India

In India, unlike in the US, there is **no tax advantage for ETFs**. A Nifty 50 ETF and a Nifty 50 index fund are taxed the same way. Indian funds do not pass capital gains on to investors every year; you pay tax only when you sell, and on dividends you receive (added to your income at your slab rate). The rules since the July 2024 Budget:

| Fund type | Short-term (rate) | Long-term (holding period and rate) |
|---|---|---|
| Equity funds and equity ETFs (at least 65% in Indian shares) | Held 12 months or less: 20% | More than 12 months: 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year |
| Gold and silver ETFs | Held 12 months or less: slab rate | More than 12 months: 12.5% |
| Gold and silver mutual funds (fund of funds) | Held 24 months or less: slab rate | More than 24 months: 12.5% |
| Debt funds and debt ETFs bought on or after 1 April 2023 | Slab rate, however long you hold | Not applicable |

Cess (and surcharge, where it applies) is added on top. ELSS (tax-saving) funds give a deduction only if you use the old tax regime; there is no ELSS ETF.

## When to choose a mutual fund

- **You want automatic SIPs** of a fixed amount every month without placing orders.
- **You don't have a demat account** and don't want one.
- **You want active management**, sector or thematic funds, hybrid funds, debt funds for specific durations, or ELSS for tax saving under the old regime.
- **You invest small amounts often**, where per-trade charges on ETFs would add up.

## When to choose an ETF

- **You already have a demat account** and are comfortable placing orders.
- **You want the lowest possible running cost** on a large, long-term investment in a popular index.
- **You want to buy at a known price** during market hours, for example when investing a lump sum.
- **You want gold or silver exposure** through a listed product with a 12-month long-term holding period.

## A simple example

Say you want to put **₹10,000** into the Nifty 50.

- **Nifty 50 index fund (direct plan):** you invest exactly ₹10,000 at that day's NAV, with no demat account and no brokerage. You can set it up as a ₹10,000 monthly SIP in a few minutes.
- **Nifty 50 ETF:** you buy as many whole units as ₹10,000 allows at the live price, through your broker. You may pay small transaction charges, and a little money may be left over because you can only buy whole units. In return, the ETF's expense ratio is often a little lower.

Over 10 to 20 years, both should deliver very similar returns before costs. The cheaper option overall depends on the expense ratios on the day you invest and how often you buy.

## Which is better?

Neither is better for everyone. If you are starting out, a low-cost **index fund through SIP** is the simplest route. If you already trade shares and invest larger lump sums, a liquid, low-cost **ETF** can save you a little every year. Many investors use both: ETFs or index funds for the core of the portfolio and a few active mutual funds where they believe a manager adds value. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can ETFs beat mutual funds in returns?

An ETF and an index fund tracking the same index give almost the same returns; the small difference comes from costs and tracking error. Compared with actively managed funds, low-cost index products often do well over long periods because of their lower fees, but past results are no guarantee.

### Are ETFs riskier than mutual funds?

Not by themselves. The risk depends on what the fund holds: a Nifty 50 ETF and a Nifty 50 index fund carry the same market risk. ETFs add liquidity risk, because a thinly traded ETF can be hard to sell at a fair price.

### Do I need a demat account to buy ETFs in India?

Yes. ETFs are listed on the NSE and BSE, so you need a demat and trading account with a broker. Mutual funds, including index funds, can be bought without a demat account.

### Do ETFs pay dividends?

Some do, but many Indian ETFs keep the dividends from their holdings inside the fund, where they show up in the NAV; check the scheme document. Any dividend you do receive from an ETF or mutual fund is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate.
