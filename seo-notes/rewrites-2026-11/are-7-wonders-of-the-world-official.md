Are the Seven Wonders of the World official? **No.** There is no single official list approved by any government, the United Nations or UNESCO. What exists are two famous lists: the **Seven Wonders of the Ancient World**, handed down by Greek writers more than 2,000 years ago, and the **New Seven Wonders of the World**, picked in a privately run public vote announced in 2007.

**Quick answer:** neither list is "official" in a legal or institutional sense. The ancient list became traditional through Greek writers such as **Antipater of Sidon** and **Philo of Byzantium**. The 2007 list, which includes India's **Taj Mahal**, came from a campaign by the Swiss-based **New7Wonders** organisation, and **UNESCO publicly distanced itself** from it. If you want an official, expert-run recognition of the world's most important sites, that is UNESCO's World Heritage List, which has hundreds of sites, not seven.

## Who made the original Seven Wonders list?

The idea of listing "wonders" comes from ancient Greek travel writing. The Greeks called them *themata*, "things to be seen", much like a must-see list for travellers in the Greek world.

- **Herodotus** (5th century BCE), often called the father of history, described many marvels in his *Histories*, including the pyramids and Babylon, but he did not compile the famous seven.
- **Callimachus of Cyrene** (3rd century BCE) wrote a collection of wonders, but it is lost.
- **Antipater of Sidon**, a poet of the 2nd century BCE, wrote a poem naming seven wonders. His version listed the **walls of Babylon** instead of the Lighthouse of Alexandria.
- **Philo of Byzantium** is credited with a short work *On the Seven Wonders*, which also includes Babylon's walls.

The list most people learn today, with the Lighthouse of Alexandria, settled over later centuries. So the ancient wonders are a **traditional** list, not an official one.

## The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World

| Wonder | Where it stood (modern country) | What happened to it |
|---|---|---|
| Great Pyramid of Giza | Egypt | Still standing, the only survivor |
| Hanging Gardens of Babylon | Near Hillah, Iraq | No trace found; some historians doubt they existed |
| Temple of Artemis at Ephesus | Near Selçuk, Turkey | Burned in 356 BCE, rebuilt, later destroyed |
| Statue of Zeus at Olympia | Greece | Lost, probably destroyed by fire in the 5th century CE |
| Mausoleum at Halicarnassus | Bodrum, Turkey | Ruined by earthquakes; stones reused in a castle in the 1490s |
| Colossus of Rhodes | Rhodes, Greece | Toppled by an earthquake in 226 BCE |
| Lighthouse of Alexandria | Egypt | Damaged by earthquakes and gone by the late Middle Ages |

Note that **Ephesus and Halicarnassus are in today's Turkey**, not Greece, even though they were Greek cities in ancient times.

## The New Seven Wonders of the World (2007)

In 1999, Swiss adventurer **Bernard Weber** launched a campaign to choose new wonders by public vote. People voted online and by phone, and the organisers said around **100 million votes** were cast. The results were announced at a ceremony in **Lisbon, Portugal, on 7 July 2007**.

The New Seven Wonders, in no ranked order:

1. **Great Wall of China** (China)
2. **Petra** (Jordan)
3. **Christ the Redeemer** (Rio de Janeiro, Brazil)
4. **Machu Picchu** (Peru)
5. **Chichén Itzá** (Mexico)
6. **Colosseum** (Rome, Italy)
7. **Taj Mahal** (Agra, India)

The **Pyramids of Giza** were given **honorary** status rather than being made to compete, after Egyptian officials objected to the oldest wonder being put to a popularity vote.

India's entry, the Taj Mahal, was built by Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in memory of his wife Mumtaz Mahal. Our [facts about the Taj Mahal](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/facts-about-taj-mahal) cover its history, myths and visiting tips.

## Why the New Seven Wonders are not "official"

The 2007 list is popular, and many tourism boards use the title, but it has no official standing:

- **It was a private campaign.** New7Wonders is a private Swiss-based organisation, not a government or UN body.
- **UNESCO did not take part.** In a statement the day before the results, UNESCO called the campaign a private undertaking, said the list reflected only the views of people with internet access, and said it would not help protect the sites.
- **Voting favoured big countries and campaigns.** Countries with large online populations and strong national campaigns could push their sites up, so the result measured popularity, not historical or architectural importance.
- **It was not a scientific poll.** Anyone could take part, so the result measured enthusiasm, not a representative sample of the world.

None of this makes the seven sites less impressive. It only means the title "wonder of the world" is a label, not an official status.

## Is there an official list of world heritage?

The closest thing to an official, expert list is **UNESCO's World Heritage List**, set up under the 1972 World Heritage Convention. Sites are nominated by governments and evaluated by expert advisory bodies before the World Heritage Committee decides. All seven New Wonders, the Pyramids of Giza and many Indian sites such as the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort and the Ajanta and Ellora caves are on it. You can browse it on the [UNESCO World Heritage Centre website](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/).

## Other "wonders" lists

The success of the 2007 vote led New7Wonders to run more campaigns, including the **New7Wonders of Nature** (results in 2011) and **New7Wonders Cities** (2014). Media outlets and tourism bodies in many countries have also run their own "seven wonders" polls. All of these are popularity contests, not official designations.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the Seven Wonders of the World official?

No. There is no official list recognised by any government or by UNESCO. The ancient list is a tradition handed down by Greek writers, and the 2007 New Seven Wonders came from a privately run public vote that UNESCO distanced itself from.

### What are the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World?

They are the Great Pyramid of Giza, the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the Statue of Zeus at Olympia, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, the Colossus of Rhodes and the Lighthouse of Alexandria. Only the Great Pyramid of Giza still stands.

### How were the New Seven Wonders chosen?

They were chosen through a global online and phone vote run by the Swiss-based New7Wonders campaign, launched in 1999. The winners, including the Taj Mahal, were announced in Lisbon on 7 July 2007, and the Pyramids of Giza were given honorary status.

### Who wrote the first list of the Seven Wonders?

No single author can be named with certainty. The classic list is usually credited to the Greek poet Antipater of Sidon (2nd century BCE) and to Philo of Byzantium. Herodotus, who lived in the 5th century BCE, wrote about many marvels but did not compile the famous seven.
