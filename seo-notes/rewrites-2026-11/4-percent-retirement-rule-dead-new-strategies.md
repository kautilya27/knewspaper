**Quick answer:** The 4% rule is not dead, but it is a rough American guideline, not a safe plan for most young Indians. It says you can withdraw 4% of your retirement corpus in the first year and raise that amount with inflation every year after, and your money should last about 30 years. India's higher inflation, longer retirements (especially if you retire early) and the lack of a state pension for most private-sector workers mean a starting withdrawal of about **3% to 3.5%** is a more cautious target, combined with a flexible plan.

For Gen Z and millennials, the bigger point is this: you have 25 to 35 years to build the corpus, and the rule is more useful as a **savings target** (save 25 to 33 times your yearly expenses) than as a fixed withdrawal formula. Below are worked ₹ examples, why the rule struggles in India, and the strategies that work better with EPF, PPF, NPS and mutual fund SWPs. Rates were checked on 5 November 2026.

## What is the 4% rule?

The rule comes from a 1994 study by US financial planner William Bengen. He tested past US stock and bond returns and found that a retiree who withdrew 4% of the portfolio in year one, then increased the rupee (in his case, dollar) amount with inflation each year, would not have run out of money over any 30-year period in his data. Later research such as the 1998 "Trinity study" reached similar conclusions.

**Worked example in rupees:**

- Retirement corpus: **₹2 crore**
- Year 1 withdrawal at 4%: **₹8,00,000** (about ₹66,667 a month)
- If inflation is 6%, year 2 withdrawal: ₹8,00,000 × 1.06 = **₹8,48,000**
- Year 3: ₹8,48,000 × 1.06 = **₹8,98,880**
- By year 13, at 6% inflation, you would be withdrawing about **₹16.1 lakh** a year, double the first year, just to buy the same things.

You do not recalculate 4% of whatever is left each year. You keep withdrawing the inflation-adjusted amount, whatever the market does. That is exactly why the rule can fail when inflation is high or markets fall early in retirement.

Even in the US, the number is debated. Bengen himself now suggests about 4.7% based on newer data, while Morningstar's recent research puts a safe starting rate for US retirees closer to 3.9%. Neither figure was worked out for Indian markets, Indian inflation or Indian taxes.

## Why the 4% rule is risky for young Indians

### 1. Indian inflation is higher and less predictable

The RBI targets 4% consumer inflation with a band of 2% to 6%. Actual inflation has swung widely: it fell below 1% in late 2025 and was about 5.5% in September 2026. Over a 30-year retirement, long-term planners usually assume around 6% for household costs, and medical costs often rise faster. When inflation eats most of your return, the corpus shrinks quickly.

### 2. Retirements are getting longer

If you follow FIRE (financial independence, retire early) and stop working at 45, your money may need to last 40 years or more, not 30.

### 3. No state pension for most private-sector workers

The US rule assumes retirees also get Social Security. In India, most private-sector and gig workers rely only on their own savings: EPF, NPS, PPF and investments. There is no backup income if the corpus runs out.

### 4. The order of returns matters

A market crash in the first few years of retirement, while you are withdrawing, does far more damage than the same crash 15 years later. This is called sequence-of-returns risk.

### A simple illustration

Assume a ₹2 crore corpus that earns a steady 7% a year, with withdrawals rising at 6% inflation (tax ignored, returns kept artificially smooth):

| Starting withdrawal | Year 1 amount | Roughly how long the money lasts |
|---|---|---|
| 4% | ₹8,00,000 | About 29 years |
| 3.5% | ₹7,00,000 | About 34 years |
| 3% | ₹6,00,000 | About 40 years |

Real markets are bumpier, so treat this only as a way to see how much difference half a percentage point makes.

## How much corpus do you need?

Use your expected yearly expenses at retirement and divide by your withdrawal rate:

| Withdrawal rate | Multiple of yearly expenses | Corpus for ₹6 lakh a year (₹50,000 a month) |
|---|---|---|
| 4% | 25 times | ₹1.5 crore |
| 3.5% | about 28.6 times | about ₹1.71 crore |
| 3% | about 33 times | ₹2 crore |

**Do not forget inflation before retirement.** If you are 30 and spend ₹50,000 a month today, the same lifestyle at 60 will cost about **₹2.87 lakh a month** (about ₹34.5 lakh a year) at 6% inflation. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, that means a corpus of roughly **₹9.8 crore** in future rupees. The number looks huge, but you also have 30 years of compounding on your side.

For example, a SIP of **₹10,000 a month for 30 years** grows to about **₹2.1 crore** if it earns 10% a year, and about **₹3.1 crore** at 12% a year. These returns are assumptions, not promises; equity returns vary a lot from year to year. Raising the SIP as your salary grows (a "step-up" SIP) closes the gap much faster. ## Better retirement strategies for Gen Z and millennials in India

### 1. Start lower and stay flexible

Plan for a 3% to 3.5% starting withdrawal. In retirement, use simple guardrails: skip the inflation increase in a year when the market has fallen sharply, and take a little more after strong years. Flexible spending protects the corpus far better than any fixed rule.

### 2. Build the corpus with India's retirement tools

- **EPF:** If you are salaried, your EPF earns a government-declared rate (8.25% for 2025-26). You can add more through the Voluntary Provident Fund (VPF). Avoid withdrawing EPF when you change jobs; transfer it instead.
- **PPF:** 7.1% a year (October to December 2026 rate), tax-free, with a 15-year lock-in and a ₹1.5 lakh yearly limit. Good for the safe part of your plan, especially for freelancers and gig workers with no EPF.
- **NPS:** a low-cost pension scheme with equity and debt options. Under rules notified in December 2025, non-government subscribers exiting at 60 must put at least 20% of the corpus into an annuity and can take up to 80% as a lump sum or regular payouts. Under current tax rules only 60% of the corpus is tax-free on exit, so check how the rest is taxed before you withdraw it.
- **Equity mutual funds and index funds:** the main growth engine for a 25 to 35 year horizon. Read our guide on [what mutual funds are and how they are taxed](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/what-are-mutual-funds) if you are new to them.

### 3. Use buckets in retirement

Split your corpus by when you will need the money:

- **Bucket 1 (next 2 to 3 years of expenses):** savings account, fixed deposits or liquid funds.
- **Bucket 2 (years 3 to 10):** Senior Citizens Savings Scheme (8.2% for October to December 2026, up to ₹30 lakh per person), Post Office Monthly Income Scheme (7.4%), short-term debt funds or conservative hybrid funds.
- **Bucket 3 (10 years and beyond):** equity funds that can grow and refill the other buckets.

When markets fall, you spend from Bucket 1 and avoid selling equity at low prices.

### 4. Withdraw through an SWP

A **systematic withdrawal plan (SWP)** pays you a fixed amount from a mutual fund every month while the rest stays invested. It is the closest Indian product to how the 4% rule works. Tax applies only to the gains part of each withdrawal: on equity funds, long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year are taxed at 12.5% and short-term gains at 20%; gains on debt funds bought after 1 April 2023 are taxed at your slab rate. Our [SWP guide](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/what-is-swp) explains how to set one up.

### 5. Protect the plan

- Buy **health insurance** early, separate from any employer cover. One hospital bill can undo years of saving.
- Keep **term insurance** if anyone depends on your income.
- Keep an **emergency fund** of six months of expenses so you never break your retirement investments.

### 6. Keep more than one income stream

Part-time consulting, freelancing, rental income or dividends in your 50s and 60s reduce how much you need to withdraw. Even ₹20,000 a month of side income in early retirement means your corpus can be ₹80 lakh smaller at a 3% withdrawal rate.

## If you are behind on retirement savings

1. **Clear expensive debt first:** credit card dues and personal loans cost far more than investments usually earn.
2. **Automate a SIP** on salary day and raise it every year.
3. **Avoid lifestyle inflation:** when your salary rises, invest at least half of the raise.
4. **Consider working a few years longer:** every extra year adds savings and removes a year of withdrawals.

## Is the 4% rule dead?

No, but in India it is a starting point, not a promise. Use 25 times your expenses as a minimum target, aim for about 30 times if you can, keep your withdrawals flexible, and use the tools India gives you: EPF, PPF, NPS, SCSS and mutual fund SWPs. If your situation is complex, a SEBI-registered investment adviser can build a plan around your actual numbers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use the 4% rule if I retire early in India?

It is risky. An early retiree may need the money to last 40 years or more, so a starting withdrawal of about 3% (a corpus of around 33 times yearly expenses) and flexible spending are safer.

### What is a safe withdrawal rate for retirement in India?

There is no official figure. Because Indian inflation is higher than in the US, many planners use about 3% to 3.5% as a cautious starting rate, adjusted each year based on how markets and expenses behave.

### How much do I need to retire in India with ₹50,000 monthly expenses?

At today's prices, about ₹1.5 crore at a 4% withdrawal rate or ₹2 crore at 3%. If you are decades away from retirement, first increase your expenses for inflation; at 6%, ₹50,000 today becomes about ₹2.87 lakh a month in 30 years.

### Is crypto a good retirement investment?

Crypto is highly volatile and is not regulated as an investment product in India, and gains are taxed at a flat 30% (plus cess) with 1% TDS on transfers. If you hold any, keep it to a very small part of your portfolio and build your retirement on regulated products like EPF, PPF, NPS and mutual funds.
