The rupee is falling against the dollar because, over the past year, far more dollars have been leaving India than coming in. Costly oil imports, a wide trade gap and heavy selling by foreign investors have all pushed up demand for dollars, while a strong US dollar and high global bond yields have pulled money towards the US.

**Quick answer:** in 2025–2026 the rupee weakened for four main reasons: **expensive crude oil** after the West Asia conflict that began in late February 2026, **record selling of Indian shares by foreign investors**, a **wider trade deficit**, and a **strong US dollar** backed by high US bond yields. US tariffs on Indian goods added pressure in late 2025. The rupee crossed 90 per dollar for the first time in December 2025 and hit fresh record lows close to 97 in May 2026. The RBI has sold dollars from its reserves and, in October 2026, raised interest rates to slow the fall.

## How the rupee-dollar rate is decided

India has a **market-determined exchange rate**. The rupee's value moves with demand and supply in the currency market, and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) does not fix a level. The RBI steps in only to smooth sharp, disorderly moves.

Think of it as a simple balance:

- **Demand for dollars** comes from importers (crude oil, gold, electronics), Indians paying for foreign education and travel, companies repaying foreign loans, and foreign investors taking money out of India.
- **Supply of dollars** comes from exporters, IT and services firms, money sent home by Indians abroad (remittances), foreign direct investment and foreign portfolio investors buying Indian shares and bonds.

When dollar demand runs ahead of supply for weeks or months, each dollar costs more rupees, and the rupee "falls". The opposite makes the rupee rise.

## Why the rupee fell in late 2025

The slide began well before 2026. Three things weighed on the rupee through 2025:

- **US tariffs and trade-deal uncertainty.** From August 2025 the US imposed tariffs of up to 50% on many Indian goods. Exporters worried about orders, and every delay in trade talks hit sentiment. A framework for an interim India–US trade deal in February 2026 cut that tariff to 18%, which helped briefly.
- **Foreign investors selling Indian shares.** Foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) pulled out about $18.9 billion from Indian equities in 2025, according to PTI's tally of market data.
- **A strong dollar.** High US interest rates kept the dollar attractive to global investors.

On 3 December 2025 the rupee crossed **90 per dollar** for the first time, and it started 2026 at around that level.

## Why the rupee is falling in 2026

### 1. Oil prices after the West Asia conflict

India buys most of its crude oil abroad and pays for it in dollars. After the conflict involving Iran began in late February 2026 and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, oil prices jumped; Brent futures were trading around $110 a barrel in May 2026. A higher oil bill means oil companies need many more dollars every month, which drags the rupee down. The rupee broke past 95 on 30 March 2026.

### 2. Record selling by foreign investors

Foreign investors have sold almost **$30 billion** of Indian shares in 2026 up to early October, already more than in all of 2025, as reported by Business Today. They have moved money to safer assets and to markets riding the global AI boom. Each sale ends with a foreign investor converting rupees back into dollars.

### 3. A wider trade deficit

Costlier oil, along with a surge in gold imports in spring 2026, widened the gap between what India imports and exports. India's trade deficit rose to $28.4 billion in April 2026, the government's trade data showed. A wider trade gap usually means a wider **current account deficit**, which has to be financed with foreign money.

### 4. A strong dollar and high global bond yields

When US Treasury yields rise (the 30-year yield went above 5% in May 2026), investors can earn more by simply holding US government bonds. Money flows out of emerging markets such as India, and the dollar strengthens against most currencies, not just the rupee.

### 5. Inflation pressure at home

Costlier fuel and imports push up Indian inflation. If prices in India rise faster than in the US for long, the rupee tends to lose value over time to keep Indian goods competitive.

By early October 2026 the rupee had fallen more than 7% for the year and was trading close to its May record low.

## What the RBI is doing about it

The RBI's aim is to stop panicky, one-way moves, not to defend a fixed number. In 2026 it has:

- **Sold dollars from its reserves** in the spot and forward markets. India's foreign exchange reserves hit a record of about $785 billion in early September 2026 and fell to about $734 billion by 2 October, RBI data showed, which still covers roughly 11 months of imports.
- **Curbed speculation**, for example by capping banks' net open positions in the rupee at $100 million from April 2026.
- **Raised interest rates.** In October 2026 the Monetary Policy Committee lifted the repo rate by 0.25 percentage points to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023, citing inflation from oil and pressure on the rupee. It also changed its stance to "calibrated tightening".

Governor Sanjay Malhotra has said reserves are adequate and that the rupee may be undervalued. You can follow official policy statements and reserves data on the [Reserve Bank of India's website](https://www.rbi.org.in).

The RBI cannot hold the rupee up forever. Reserves are finite, and selling too many dollars drains money from the banking system. That is why it mixes intervention with interest rates and rules on speculation.

## Is a falling rupee always bad?

No. A gradual fall helps exporters compete and supports jobs in sectors such as IT services, textiles and engineering goods. The problem is a **fast, disorderly fall**, which raises import costs and inflation, makes foreign loans costlier to repay and can scare investors further. That is why the RBI focuses on the speed of the fall more than the level.

Some people ask why the government doesn't simply print more rupees or more dollars to fix this. Printing money usually makes inflation worse and the currency weaker; our explainer on [why we can't print more money](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/why-cant-we-print-more-money) covers this.

## What could help the rupee recover

Analysts generally point to the same few triggers:

- Lower crude oil prices and calmer conditions in West Asia.
- Foreign investors returning to Indian shares and bonds.
- A narrower trade and current account deficit.
- A weaker US dollar, for example if the US Federal Reserve cuts rates.

None of these is certain, and nobody can reliably predict currency levels. For the latest reference rate, check the RBI or a major financial news site rather than old articles.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who decides the value of the rupee against the dollar?

The rupee's exchange rate is set mainly by demand and supply in the currency market, not by the government. The Reserve Bank of India does not fix a rate, but it buys or sells dollars from its foreign exchange reserves to smooth out sharp swings in either direction.

### How does a falling rupee affect common people?

A weaker rupee makes anything priced in dollars cost more in India. That includes imported crude oil, which can push up fuel and transport costs, as well as gold, electronics, foreign travel and fees for studying abroad. These higher costs can feed into overall inflation over time.

### Who benefits when the rupee falls against the dollar?

Exporters and companies that earn in dollars, such as IT services firms, gain because each dollar they earn converts into more rupees. Indians working abroad and their families also benefit, since money sent home is worth more in rupees. Foreign tourists visiting India find their trip cheaper too.

### Can the RBI stop the rupee from falling?

The RBI can slow a fall but cannot hold the rupee at one level forever. It can sell dollars from its reserves, raise interest rates, as it did in October 2026, and curb speculation, but its reserves are limited and its main aim is to prevent disorderly swings rather than defend a fixed rate.
