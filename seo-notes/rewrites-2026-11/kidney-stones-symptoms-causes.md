A kidney stone can sit inside the kidney for months or even years without causing any symptoms. Then it moves, and many people describe the pain as one of the worst they have felt. Because the change from no symptoms to severe pain can happen within hours, it helps to know what kidney stones are, what causes them and which symptoms need urgent care.

**Quick answer:** the main symptom of a kidney stone is severe, cramping pain in the side or lower back that comes in waves and can spread to the lower abdomen and groin, often with nausea, vomiting, painful urination or blood in the urine. Stones form when minerals in concentrated urine crystallise, most often because of too little fluid, diet, certain medical conditions or family history. **Pain with fever or chills needs emergency care.**

This guide covers what kidney stones are, how they feel, the main types, causes and risk factors, and how they are diagnosed, treated and prevented. It is general information, not a substitute for seeing a doctor.

## What are kidney stones?

Kidney stones are hard deposits of minerals and salts that form inside the kidneys when urine contains more of these substances than it can dissolve. Doctors call the condition nephrolithiasis or urolithiasis. Stones range from the size of a grain of sand to, occasionally, a stone large enough to fill much of the kidney's drainage system, known as a staghorn stone.

**Small stones often pass out in the urine, sometimes without the person ever knowing.** Larger stones, or stones that get stuck in the ureter (the tube that carries urine from the kidney to the bladder), can cause severe pain and may need treatment.

## Symptoms of kidney stones

A stone that stays still inside the kidney often causes no symptoms. Symptoms usually begin when the stone moves into the ureter and blocks the flow of urine.

### Renal colic: the typical kidney stone pain

**Renal colic is a severe, cramping pain in the side and back, below the ribs.** It tends to come in waves, getting worse and easing over minutes. As the stone moves, the pain often spreads towards the lower abdomen and groin, and in men to the testicles. Many people cannot find a comfortable position and keep shifting or pacing, which is different from many other kinds of abdominal pain.

### Other symptoms

- Pain or burning when passing urine, especially as the stone nears the bladder
- Urine that is pink, red or brown, or cloudy and foul-smelling
- Needing to pass urine more often or urgently
- Nausea and vomiting, which are common with severe pain
- Passing only small amounts of urine, or difficulty passing urine

### When symptoms suggest infection

**Fever, chills or shivering together with kidney stone symptoms need urgent medical attention.** They can mean the blocked kidney is infected. An infected, blocked kidney can become serious quickly and should never be managed at home.

## The main types of kidney stones

The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kidney-stones)) describes **four main types**: calcium stones, uric acid stones, struvite stones and cystine stones. Calcium stones come in two forms, calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate, which is why the table below has five rows.

| Type | How common | Main causes |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate (calcium stone) | The most common type | High oxalate or calcium in urine, low fluid intake, diet, some bowel conditions |
| Calcium phosphate (calcium stone) | Less common than calcium oxalate; can occur alongside it | Some metabolic conditions, such as renal tubular acidosis, and some medicines |
| Uric acid | Less common | Acidic urine, a diet high in purines (red meat, organ meat, shellfish), gout, dehydration |
| Struvite | Less common | Urinary tract infections; can grow large quickly |
| Cystine | Rare | An inherited condition called cystinuria |

Knowing your stone type matters because prevention is type-specific. Someone with uric acid stones needs different changes from someone with calcium oxalate stones. **If you pass a stone, try to catch it (for example, by passing urine through a strainer or gauze) and take it to your doctor for analysis.**

## What causes kidney stones?

Stones form when the concentration of certain minerals in the urine rises to the point where they start to crystallise and stick together. Several factors contribute.

### Not drinking enough fluid

Low fluid intake is the most consistent risk factor. Concentrated urine is ideal for crystals to form. **People who live in hot climates, work outdoors or sweat heavily during exercise are at higher risk** because they lose more fluid, which is relevant for much of India, especially in summer.

### Diet

- **Salt:** a high-salt diet raises the amount of calcium in the urine.
- **Animal protein:** large amounts of meat, fish and eggs increase uric acid and calcium in the urine.
- **Oxalate-rich foods:** foods such as spinach, nuts, chocolate and tea can contribute to calcium oxalate stones in people who are prone to them.
- **Low fibre:** a high-protein, low-fibre diet is one of the risk factors the NHS lists for stones coming back.

### Medical conditions

Conditions that raise the risk include:

- Hyperparathyroidism, which raises calcium levels in the blood and urine
- Gout, which raises uric acid
- Inflammatory bowel disease and other bowel conditions that change how oxalate is absorbed
- Renal tubular acidosis and some other metabolic conditions
- Obesity and metabolic syndrome
- Repeated urinary tract infections, especially for struvite stones
- Cystic kidney disease or a blockage in the urinary tract

### Medicines and supplements

Some medicines taken long-term can raise the risk, including certain diuretics, calcium-based antacids, the HIV drug indinavir and the anti-seizure drug topiramate. High-dose vitamin C supplements can also increase oxalate in the urine. **Tell any doctor who prescribes a new medicine that you have had kidney stones**, and don't stop a prescribed medicine without advice.

### Family history and genetics

Having a close relative with kidney stones makes you more likely to get them. Rare inherited conditions, such as cystinuria and primary hyperoxaluria, cause stones that start early in life and keep coming back. Men also get kidney stones more often than women.

## How kidney stones are diagnosed

If a doctor suspects a kidney stone, they will usually arrange some of these tests:

- **Urine tests** to look for blood, crystals and signs of infection
- **Blood tests** to check kidney function and levels of calcium, uric acid and other substances
- **Imaging:** a CT scan without contrast is the most accurate test. Ultrasound is often used instead, especially in pregnancy and in children, to avoid radiation. Plain X-rays show some types of stone but miss others.
- **Stone analysis** if you pass a stone, to find out its type
- For people with repeated stones, a **24-hour urine collection** may be used to find the cause

## Treatment options

Treatment depends on the size and position of the stone, how much pain you have and whether there is a blockage or infection. Your doctor will decide which option suits you.

### Small stones: waiting for the stone to pass

Most small stones pass on their own. According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/15604-kidney-stones), around 90% of stones smaller than about 6 mm pass without a procedure, and stones under 4 mm may pass within one to two weeks; larger ones can take longer. Treatment at this stage usually means pain relief, drinking enough fluid and sometimes a medicine that relaxes the ureter to help the stone pass. Your doctor may arrange a follow-up scan to make sure it has passed. See a doctor again if the stone has not passed within a few weeks or the pain gets worse.

### Larger stones or blockages

Larger stones are less likely to pass on their own and often need a procedure:

- **Shock wave lithotripsy (SWL):** shock waves from outside the body break the stone into small pieces that can pass in the urine. It is usually done as a day procedure.
- **Ureteroscopy:** a thin telescope is passed through the urethra and bladder into the ureter or kidney, and the stone is removed or broken up, often with a laser.
- **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL):** used for large stones. Instruments are passed into the kidney through a small cut in the back to break up and remove the stone.
- **Open surgery:** now rarely needed, because the minimally invasive methods above work for most stones.

If a blocked kidney is infected, doctors may first place a small tube (a stent) or a drain to relieve the blockage and treat the infection before removing the stone.

Uric acid stones are a partial exception to the rule that stones don't dissolve: medicines that make the urine less acidic can sometimes dissolve them over time, under a doctor's care.

## How to prevent kidney stones

**Drinking enough fluid is the most important step.** The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/kidney-stones/) advises adults who are prone to stones to drink 2.5 to 3 litres of water spread through the day, and more in hot weather or when exercising. Pale yellow urine is a good sign; dark urine means you need to drink more.

- **Cut down on salt.** The WHO recommends less than 5 g of salt a day for adults. Watch for hidden salt in pickles, papad, namkeen and processed foods.
- **Moderate animal protein**, especially red meat, organ meat and shellfish.
- **Don't cut out calcium-rich foods** such as milk, curd and paneer unless your doctor tells you to. Getting enough calcium from food helps bind oxalate in the gut; calcium supplements are a different matter, so check with your doctor before taking them.
- **Avoid high-dose vitamin C supplements** unless prescribed.
- **Keep a healthy weight**, and be active.
- **Lemon juice in water** is one of the NHS's suggestions; citrate in lemon can help prevent some stones.
- **Limit fizzy, caffeinated and alcoholic drinks.** Beer is sometimes suggested as a home remedy to "flush out" stones, but alcohol can dehydrate you; see our article on [whether beer is good for health](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/is-beer-good-for-health).

If you have had stones before, follow the diet advice your doctor gives for your stone type. People with repeated stones are sometimes prescribed medicines that reduce the substances that form stones or make the urine less acidic; take these only on a doctor's advice. For general healthy-eating principles, see our guide to [a well-balanced diet](https://www.knewspaper.in/post/the-importance-of-a-well-balanced-diet).

## When to see a doctor or go to the emergency department

See a doctor promptly if you think you have a kidney stone. **Go to the emergency department straight away** if you have:

- Severe pain in the side or back that painkillers do not control
- Pain with fever, chills or shivering
- Vomiting that stops you keeping fluids down
- Very little or no urine, or great difficulty passing urine
- Kidney stone symptoms and only one kidney, a kidney transplant, or pregnancy

**Fever with kidney stone pain is a medical emergency.** Do not wait to see whether it settles. A blocked, infected kidney can get worse quickly and needs urgent treatment.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to pass a kidney stone?

It depends on the stone's size and position. Stones under about 4 mm often pass within one to two weeks, and larger ones can take two to three weeks or more. If a stone has not passed within four to six weeks, or the pain is getting worse, see your doctor.

### Can drinking more water dissolve a kidney stone?

No. Drinking more fluid helps stop new stones forming and can help a small stone pass, but it will not dissolve a stone that has already formed. Uric acid stones are a partial exception, since medicines that make urine less acidic can sometimes dissolve them under a doctor's care.

### What foods should I avoid to prevent kidney stones?

It depends on the type of stone. For calcium oxalate stones, doctors often advise cutting down on salt and moderating oxalate-rich foods such as spinach, nuts, chocolate and tea, while still eating enough calcium from food. For uric acid stones, cutting down on red meat, organ meat and shellfish helps. Ask your doctor or a dietitian for advice based on your stone type.

### Can kidney stones come back?

Yes. Having had one kidney stone makes you more likely to get another. Finding out your stone type, drinking enough fluid and following your doctor's diet or medicine advice lowers the risk.
